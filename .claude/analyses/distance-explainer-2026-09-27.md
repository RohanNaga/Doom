## 1. What D is, step by step

1. **A map is a cloud of frames.** Every recorded tic of a map has an SD 1 latent (4 x 32 x 40). We pool each latent to 192 numbers (4 channels x 6 x 8 blocks) so a frame is a point in a 192-dimensional space that encodes what the frame looks like: walls, floor, lighting, enemies. No time coordinate is kept.
2. **Target clouds against the full reference.** For a target map we draw 250 frames per episode from 4 episodes (a 1,000-frame cloud), 25 per motion decile so still and moving frames are both represented, and average over ten such clouds (arranged as five disjoint pairs; episodes may repeat across pairs). Each training map's reference is all 12,500 frames of 50 episodes. So a small target cloud is compared with the large corpus directly, and the answer to "how can a 1,000-frame cloud be compared with a 12,500-frame corpus" is the floor (step 6): the floor clouds are 1,000-frame draws of training-map footage compared with the same 12,500-frame references, so whatever bias the unequal sizes put into the estimate is in the floor too, and only distances above the floor mean anything.
3. **Motion weights.** Each frame is weighted by how much the latent moved into it, with a floor so the quietest half of a cloud still holds a quarter of the weight. A map's shift should be measured where the game happens, not in the frames where the player stands still; this is the rule from the NVIDIA internship method carried over. `D_uniform` (no weights) is stored beside it and differs by at most 0.006 on the 13 arenas, so the weighting changes nothing here.
4. **Sliced Wasserstein-2 between two weighted clouds.** Project both clouds onto 1,000 shared random unit directions (seed 0, the same for every map), integrate the squared difference between the two weighted quantile functions exactly on each projection, and take the square root of the mean cost over directions. This is the sliced Wasserstein distance of Rabin, Peyre, Delon and Bernot (SSVM 2011) and Bonneel et al. (JMIV 2015); Kolouri et al. (NeurIPS 2019, 1902.00434) is the generalised form, of which we use the plain linear case. Its sample complexity does not depend on the dimension under mild conditions (Nadjahi et al., NeurIPS 2020, 2003.05783), which is why it is usable at 1,000 points in 192 dimensions where the full Wasserstein distance is not; that result is about i.i.d. samples, and ours are weighted frames from correlated episodes, so it is a reason for the choice, not a guarantee of accuracy.
5. **Nearest training map, not the pooled corpus.** D is the distance to the nearest of the four training maps (the minimum over maps for each cloud, averaged over the ten clouds). Distance to the pooled corpus charges a map for not also looking like the other three training maps; `D_pooled` is stored beside D and differs from it by −0.024 to +0.073 (larger on eleven arenas, smaller on arenas 8 and 11). Ten arenas pick map 3 on every cloud, arena 8 picks map 4, arena 11 picks map 2, and arena 16 splits five clouds each between maps 2 and 3.
6. **The floor.** What is "zero shift" in this estimator? A training map against a second, disjoint draw of its own episodes (`floor_draw 2`), which gives 0.022 to 0.093 over 40 such comparisons (mean 0.044). Any map inside that band is indistinguishable from a resampling of training footage. The training maps' held-out validation episodes score 0.026 to 0.069 (inside the floor, as they must); every unseen arena scores 0.118 to 0.270 (outside).
7. **Uncertainty.** Two bootstraps are stored: `bootstrap_full` (200 draws, each redrawing the map's episodes and averaging ten clouds as D does) gives standard deviations of 0.001 to 0.005 per arena; the older `bootstrap` (one cloud per draw) gives 0.002 to 0.011. Neither resamples the reference, the frames within episodes or the projection directions. Either way the noise is ten to a hundred times smaller than the spread between arenas (0.118 to 0.270), so D is a precise measurement; whatever it fails to predict, it does not fail from noise.

## 2. Why this distance and not another

- **Model-free and pre-computable.** D needs the VAE and the footage, no world model, so a reader can compute it for a new map before deciding whether to adapt. That is the whole point of a distance as a planning tool, and it is why the model-side quantities (zero-shot skill, the deficit Δ) are reported as a separate thing.
- **In the model's own input space.** The clouds are SD 1 latents, the space the U-Net and PixArt read (SD 3.5 reads its own 16-channel space; a D in that space is a one-command rerun), so "far" means far as those models see it, not as a pixel histogram sees it. XEWorld (2608.05799, preprint) measures appearance shift with colour histograms and shape moments and reports it against held-out-robot error; ours is a latent-space distribution distance in the same role.
- **A distribution distance, not a nearest-neighbour coverage.** The internship method scored per-scenario coverage of a test set by the training set; here maps of one WAD share appearance so densely that coverage saturates (this is what the directed-coverage candidate showed on Sep 27: training maps' held-out episodes are as "uncovered" as unseen arenas). A transport distance between clouds measures how the whole distribution of looks differs, which is the quantity that separates the families.
- **A transport distance on the empirical clouds rather than a Gaussian fit or a kernel.** FID (1706.08500) and FVD (1812.01717, preprint) are the Wasserstein-2 distance between Gaussian fits of two feature clouds, so they see only means and covariances; MMD needs a kernel bandwidth. Sliced Wasserstein uses the full empirical distributions with no parametric fit and no bandwidth. Optimal-transport distances between datasets are the established form of dataset-to-dataset distance in transfer learning (OTDD, Alvarez-Melis and Fusi, NeurIPS 2020, 2002.02923, which additionally transports label distributions); D is in that spirit for unlabelled footage, not a variant of it.
- **Nearest map rather than mixture.** A design choice of ours: a target is as far as its closest training map, because the model needs only one map it has seen to render a look. Westny et al. (2606.30777, ECCV 2026) also compute directed dataset distances pairwise (a KL divergence between Gaussian fits), but do not take a minimum over sources.

## 4. The outlier: why arena 7 starts far and improves fast

Arena 7 is the farthest map by appearance (D 0.270, lava floors and a different wall set; the figure-1 strips show the model's textures drifting toward the training maps' brick). Its zero-shot *decoded* advantage is the second-lowest of the 13 (1.070 dB, above arena 6's 1.069), but its zero-shot *latent* skill is the median (1.42, rank 7 of 13). That is the signature of an appearance shift rather than a dynamics shift: the model's latent predictions on arena 7 are as good as elsewhere relative to copy-last, but when rendered they pay in pixels for every wrong texture, so the decoded advantage collapses. Appearance is also the cheapest thing for an adapter to learn: the adapter (8 episodes; on the ladder one episode already gives 91 percent of its gain) recovers 2.8 dB and crosses at 250 updates, and ends mid-pack (3.86), exactly where its latent skill said it belonged. Arena 6 (D 0.198, A0 1.07, S0 1.14, the lowest skill) is the other far map, and it is different: it is hard in latent space too, so it starts low for two reasons and crosses only at 1,000.

So the outlier is not a failure of D; it is the one arena where D measures the thing that matters (appearance), because it is the only arena whose appearance shift is large. On the other eleven, appearance shift is small and similar (D 0.118 to 0.184), and what differs between them is how predictable their play is, which no appearance distance can see and which the zero-shot skill measures directly. This reading is a hypothesis with one supporting arena; the October constructed-shift experiment (retexture a training map at several strengths) is the test.

**Arena 9 above the in-distribution line.** It is the only arena above the reference at 4k (5.11 against 5.06) and the second most predictable zero-shot (S0 1.86, A0 2.88; arena 15 is marginally higher on both, 1.96 and 2.88, and ends at 4.56). Nothing about D explains either (0.184 and 0.127, both mid-range); their predictability does.

## 5. What the paper says (and cites)

"We measure appearance shift with a frame distance D: the sliced Wasserstein-2 distance (Rabin et al. 2011; Bonneel et al. 2015) between a motion-weighted cloud of a map's per-frame SD 1 latents and the footage of its nearest training map, calibrated against a resampling floor of the training maps themselves, in the spirit of optimal-transport dataset distances (Alvarez-Melis and Fusi 2020) and in the role of the appearance distance XEWorld reports for held-out robots. D places every unseen arena outside the training floor and does not order them; the two farthest arenas start lowest and gain most, and the rest are ordered by the model's own zero-shot skill."

## 6. The open question you raised, answered as far as it can be

"How can we compare a domain shift between a large corpus and a small unknown target?" By comparing every target cloud (1,000 frames) with the full corpus of each training map (12,500 frames) under one estimator with one set of random directions, and by measuring what that unequal comparison returns when the target is itself training footage: the floor (1,000-frame clouds of training maps against the same references, 0.02 to 0.09). Only distances above the floor mean anything, and the target's small size enters through the floor's width and its own episode bootstrap. What the estimator cannot do is turn an appearance distance into a prediction of adaptation cost when the targets differ in something other than appearance; that limitation is the finding, and the model-side deficit Δ is the quantity that fills it.
