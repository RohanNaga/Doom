## 1. Decoder in the LoRA run

The adaptation claim is about the world model: does a small adapter on frozen weights, fed a few episodes, make the model predict that arena's transitions better. The decoder never sees a transition. It renders latents to pixels, on both sides of every pixel comparison the paper makes: the model's predicted latent and the copy-last latent go through the same decoder against the same rendered or true frame. Three consequences:

- **Adapting the decoder per arena would move both sides of the headline quantity at once** and could move them unequally: a decoder trained on that arena's frames learns to render the arena's textures well from the true latents, and also learns to clean up whatever systematic error the backbone makes there. That second effect is the one that spoils the measurement: the cost curve would then credit rendering repair to the adapter. With the decoder frozen at the training-map tune, the only thing that can move the curve is the latent the world model predicts.
- **The decomposition needs the decoder held still.** Control response (directional), latent prediction error (latent skill), rendering (the ceiling) are separable only if one leg does not learn while another is measured. The rendering leg is measured by the tuned decoder's ceiling on each arena, from true latents, which is already scheduled.
- **The rendering gain on offer is small and known.** The stock ceiling is flat across maps (23.9 against 23.7 dB full frame, and most of that error is the HUD); a decoder tuned on training-map frames should raise it everywhere. Whether it raises it less on unseen arenas is exactly the rendering-leg number, and per-arena decoder adaptation would answer "how much of the remaining rendering gap is arena-specific", about 0.2 dB with the stock decoder. That is an October appendix arm at one A6000-hour per arena, reported as a third decoder column, never inside the cost curve.

Precedent: GameNGen fine-tunes its decoder once on its training footage and freezes it (2408.14837, section 4.3); no world-model paper in the five memos adapts a decoder to a held-out scene; iVideoGPT adapts its tokenizer to a new domain but as part of full adaptation, not as a rendering-only step (2405.15223). So the choice with the literature behind it is: tune once on training-map frames, freeze, adapt only the world model.

## 2. Public models as baselines

What exists, from the memos: GameNGen has no released weights; two open reproductions exist, one of which is the source of the Stiegler footage we hold (its model trains on `deathmatch_simple.wad`, one map, 4-tic action repeat, its own action set). DIAMOND released a CS:GO model (different game). Oasis is Minecraft. MultiGen and PlayGen: no verified weights. So the only candidate that plays Doom is the GameNGen reproduction.

What scoring it on our arenas would show: it is a model trained on one other map, with a different action set and frame convention, run zero-shot on our arenas. That is an out-of-distribution test of their model on our data, not a baseline for our recipe: their advantage over copy-last on our arenas would be small or negative for the same reason ours shrinks, and it says nothing about which recipe is better. It would support one sentence, "a public Doom world model trained on one map also loses its advantage over copy-last on arenas it never saw", if its action space can be mapped onto our executed controls (their discrete action set is a subset of button combinations, so a mapping exists but has to be written and checked), its frame rate reconciled (4-tic repeat against our every-tic), and its resolution matched. That is a day of work with no reuse, in the week the tables are due.

Recommendation: for Sep 30, related work names the public reproductions and DIAMOND, and the paper's baseline stays copy-last persistence, which is what makes the 30-map comparison possible in the first place. For October, one appendix row: the reproduction's model scored on our four training arenas and the 13 unseen arenas with the mapping stated, presented as an external zero-shot reference, not as a recipe comparison. "How good our architecture got" against GameNGen is a cross-paper comparison on different data and the paper should not make it; the in-domain three-backbone table under one recipe is the architecture comparison we can defend.
