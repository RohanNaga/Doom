# Predicting adaptation outcome or cost from a pre-training distance: literature (2026-09-26)

Scope: prior work for the LoRA adaptation study (`docs/lora_adaptation_design_2026-09-26.html`). The claim under test is that the distance D from an unseen map to the nearest training map predicts adaptation cost: LoRA steps or episodes until one-tic gain over persistence reaches a target. Slice: work that predicts adaptation outcome or cost from a score computed before training. Papers covered in `distance-study-literature-2026-09-25.md` (OTDD Figs. 6–7, Ben-David Theorem 2, Blitzer, Cui, Deng and Zheng, Task2Vec, Guillory, Procgen, world-model tables) are not repeated.

Every number and figure reference was read from the arXiv or proceedings PDF text on 2026-09-26; venues come from the arXiv comment, PDF header or a proceedings URL. **VERIFY** marks what I could not confirm that way.

## Bottom line

- Predicting a fixed-budget fine-tuning *outcome* from a pre-training score is a mature field. It has two branches. Transferability metrics (NCE, LEEP, LogME, GBC, PACTran, OTCE, TransRate) need labelled target data and rank source models. Dataset distances (OTDD, WTE, s-OTDD, Mensink's nearest-source distance, Westny's latent KL) rank source–target pairs.
- Predicting adaptation *cost* (steps or samples to reach a threshold) across targets from a distance is rare. It appears as binned convergence curves in LEEP (Fig. 4), as theory in Ben-David et al. (Theorem 3) and Hanneke and Kpotufe (transfer exponents), and as a fitted "transfer gap" in the transfer scaling-law papers. None of them uses a generative model or a world model.
- Two independent studies find that a symmetric optimal-transport or Wasserstein distance predicts transfer markedly worse than a directed "is the target covered by the source" measure: Mensink et al. (Table 10) and Westny et al. 2026 (Table 2). Our D is symmetric sliced Wasserstein.

## A. Transferability estimation (score from one forward pass, before fine-tuning)

All of these predict target **accuracy** after a fixed fine-tuning recipe, and all except LogME's regression variant need target class labels. The Ding et al. survey (arXiv 2402.15231, Table 1) lists "target task accuracy" as the ground truth for every paradigm it covers. Its text contains no generative-model or diffusion entry (searched for "generative" and "diffusion").

What transfers to our study: LEEP's Fig. 4 is a template. It plots the gap to a reference model against training epochs, averages tasks within five score levels, and draws a zero line where the fine-tuned model matches the reference. Higher-LEEP levels reach zero sooner (§5.5). Our analogue plots gain over persistence against LoRA steps, averaged within distance terciles, with a line at the target gain. OTCE's calibration step (fit λ1, λ2, b on a few auxiliary pairs, §3) is the transferability-literature version of fitting a cost-vs-D curve on pilot maps and predicting the rest.

## C. Scaling laws for transfer and theory with a divergence term

**Hernandez et al. 2021** (arXiv [2102.01293](https://arxiv.org/abs/2102.01293), OpenAI preprint, no venue) define effective data transferred D_T, the extra target data a from-scratch model would need to match the fine-tuned one. They fit D_T = k·(D_F)^α·(N)^β (Eq. 1.1). Table 1 gives α = 0.18 for text to Python and α = 0.096 for text plus code to Python, with β = 0.38 for both. They call α "a useful measure of the directed proximity of two distributions, with smaller α indicating closer proximity" (§1). It is fitted after training, on two sources, not measured beforehand.

**Mikami et al.** (arXiv [2108.11018](https://arxiv.org/abs/2108.11018), venue **VERIFY**) fit L(n, s) = δ(γ + n^−α)·s^−β for synthetic-to-real transfer, where n is pretraining size and s is fine-tuning size (Eq. 2). They name the constant floor the "transfer gap" C and report that it shrinks with model size (Fig. 4) and mostly with data complexity (§4.5). **Barnett** (arXiv [2408.16947](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.16947), Epoch AI report) refits the same form, L(p, f) = (A·p^−α + G)·f^−β + E, across several fine-tuning datasets. He interprets G as a directed distance between distributions (§5.1) and finds "significant variations in the transfer gap across distributions" (abstract). For us, the useful move is to fit gain(f) per map on the data curve and read G off as a threshold-free adaptation cost.

**Isik et al.** (ICLR 2025, arXiv [2402.04177](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.04177)) score alignment as language overlap (Definition 1). With A = 1, BLEU and COMET rise monotonically with pretraining data and follow a log-law. With A = 0.7 they fluctuate or fall while downstream cross-entropy keeps improving (Fig. 1). Distance can decouple loss from task metric, so read PSNR, LPIPS and the latent ratio separately. **Zhang et al.** (ICLR 2024, arXiv [2402.17193](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.17193)) fit a multiplicative law between fine-tuning data and each other factor (Eq. 1). They report that "PET parameter scaling is generally ineffective" for prompt tuning and LoRA in bilingual LLMs (abstract), a prior for the rank ablation (Decision 3).

**Theory.**
- *Ben-David et al.* (Machine Learning 79, 2010; [PDF](http://www.alexkulesza.com/pubs/adapt_mlj10.pdf)), Theorem 3: with m_T target and m_S source labels and a weight α on target error, the bound has a complexity term and a term (1 − α)·A, where A is the divergence plus λ. The optimal weight is α* = 1, meaning source data are ignored, once m_T ≥ D² with D = √d / A (§6, Eq. 2). Fig. 3 shows empirically that more distant sources give higher target error, and that more target data raise the optimal α. The number of target samples at which pretraining stops helping falls as the divergence grows. This is a cost-vs-distance prediction, stated as a phase transition.
- *Mansour, Mohri and Rostamizadeh* (COLT 2009, [proceedings PDF](https://www.cs.mcgill.ca/~colt2009/papers/003.pdf), arXiv [0902.3430](https://arxiv.org/abs/0902.3430)) define the discrepancy distance for an arbitrary loss (Definition 4). This covers squared loss, so it is the theory that applies to a regression-like outcome such as PSNR.
- *Hanneke and Kpotufe* (NeurIPS 2019, [proceedings](https://papers.nips.cc/paper/2019/hash/b91f4f4d36fa98a94ac5584af95594a0-Abstract.html), arXiv [2002.04747](https://arxiv.org/abs/2002.04747)) define a transfer exponent ρ with C_ρ·E_P(h) ≥ E_Q(h)^ρ (Definition 2). The lower bound on target excess error is min{(d/n_P)^{1/((2−β_P)ρ)}, (d/n_Q)^{1/(2−β_Q)}} (Theorem 1), which they show is attained. Here the distance enters as an **exponent** on the sample size, not as an additive term. Target samples needed to reach a fixed error therefore grow as a power of the source data, with the power set by ρ.

## D. Adaptation curves as a function of domain distance (vision, NLP, generative)

- **Neyshabur, Sedghi and Zhang** (NeurIPS 2020, arXiv [2008.11687](https://arxiv.org/abs/2008.11687)) fine-tune from ImageNet to DomainNet real, clipart, quickdraw and CheXpert, ordered by visual distance, and plot learning curves against random init (Fig. 2). They then raise distance by block-shuffling (Fig. 3). The final-accuracy benefit shrinks with distance on real and clipart but not on quickdraw. The optimization-speed benefit "is independent from feature reuse" (§3.1). This is direct evidence that *speed* of adaptation and *final gain* can respond to distance differently.
- **Gururangan et al.** (ACL 2020, arXiv [2004.10964](https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.10964)) measure vocabulary overlap between RoBERTa's domain and four target domains (Fig. 2). They state that "the more dissimilar the domain, the higher the potential for DAPT" (§3.1); the gains are in Table 3. Four domains, no statistic. Far domains have more headroom.
- **Wang et al., Transferring GANs** (ECCV 2018, arXiv [1805.01677](https://arxiv.org/abs/1805.01677)) are the generative precedent. They use four source GANs and four targets. Fine-tuned FID is in Table 4; FID between source-*generated* samples and target data is in Table 5. They call the latter "a rough indicator of suitability rather than accurate metric" (§4.5). Fig. 1 plots FID against iterations, from scratch against pretrained. From Tables 4 and 5, my own Spearman within each target (n = 4 sources) is 0.8, 0.8, 0.8 and −0.4. Pooled over targets it is −0.07 (n = 16, p = 0.80), because target difficulty dominates. This is derived by me, not reported by the paper. It is a concrete case for per-target normalization such as our persistence reference.
- **Zhao et al., AdAM** (NeurIPS 2022, [proceedings PDF](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2022/file/7b122d0a0dcb1a86ffa25ccba154652b-Paper-Conference.pdf), arXiv [2210.16559](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.16559)) measure proximity from FFHQ to six targets by FID and LPIPS (Fig. 2): Babies 147 and 0.274 up to Wild 272 and 0.484. On distant targets, few-shot GAN methods that preserve source knowledge "perform no better than a baseline fine-tuning method" (§3). For us, a frozen-backbone adapter may lose more to full fine-tuning as D grows, which would confound cost with adapter capacity.

## E. RL and world models

- **Taylor and Stone** (JMLR 10, 2009, [PDF](https://www.jmlr.org/papers/volume10/taylor09a/taylor09a.pdf)) define five transfer metrics (§2, Fig. 3): jumpstart, asymptotic performance, total reward (area under the curve), transfer ratio, and **time to threshold**. Our adaptation cost is time to threshold. They warn that it depends on the arbitrary threshold chosen and suggest comparing over a range of thresholds, which yields "a time vs. threshold curve" (§2.1).
- **Bou Ammar et al.** (AAAI 2014 per the copyright line, track **VERIFY**; [PDF](https://www.seas.upenn.edu/~eeaton/papers/BouAmmar2014Automated.pdf)) measure MDP similarity by RBM reconstruction error (RBDist) over 50 MDPs per domain on inverted pendulum, cart-pole and mountain car. They report a "strong correlation" with jumpstart (Fig. 3d–f), plotted with no statistic. The outcome is jumpstart (zero-shot), not samples to threshold.
- No paper found predicts the number of target-environment samples or updates a world model needs from an environment distance. Searches returned adaptation curves without a distance axis (AdaWorld, ICML 2025, covered by the other readers) or distance analyses outside world models (Westny). Visús et al. (arXiv 2103.04706, venue **VERIFY**) survey MDP similarity metrics without cost data.

## 1. Closest three competitors and how ours differs

1. **Westny et al. 2026, arXiv 2606.30777.** They compute a distance between learned latent embeddings of 24 trajectory datasets before training. It predicts zero-shot error (ρ = 0.811 with CI) and forgetting after fine-tuning (ρ = 0.729). They also show fine-tuning curves against training steps for three sources with different KL (Fig. 6). It is the nearest in spirit: latent space of a predictive model, a CI on Spearman, and forgetting as a second outcome. Ours differs on six points. We use a generative video world model rather than a discriminative trajectory regressor. We use a symmetric motion-weighted sliced Wasserstein distance rather than Gaussian KL. We run one source model over many target maps, not many sources for one target. Our outcome is a cost, steps or episodes to a threshold, not a fixed-budget error. We reference to copy-last-frame rather than absolute minADE. We adapt with LoRA on a frozen backbone.
2. **Nguyen et al. 2025, s-OTDD (ICML), with OTDD (NeurIPS 2020).** They use the same distance family, sliced OT between datasets, against adaptation performance, and report ρ and p per panel. Theirs is classification with labels, source–target pairs, and a fixed-budget accuracy gap. Their *NIST correlation is weak (ρ = 0.40, p = 0.08). Ours has no labels, a motion weight, a minimum over training maps, and a cost outcome.
3. **Nguyen et al. 2020, LEEP (ICML), Fig. 4, read with Mensink et al. Table 10.** LEEP is the only pre-training score whose paper shows that it orders *convergence speed* toward a reference, through curves averaged within score levels. Mensink shows that a nearest-source distance, like our minimum over maps, predicts transfer gain (τ = 0.42), better than EMD (τ = 0.20). Both are classifiers with accuracy outcomes, and LEEP's score is a model likelihood, not a data distance.

## 2. Reporting conventions to mirror

- **Headline statistic:** Spearman ρ of cost against D with a bootstrap 95% CI (Westny Table 2). This matches the design; yesterday's file covers the ρ-and-p panel style (OTDD).
- **Prediction error, not only correlation:** fit cost against D on some maps and predict the held-out ones (leave-one-map-out). Report MAE and max error against a predict-the-mean baseline (Elsahar and Gallé, Table 1). With 18 maps this is a stronger claim than ρ.
- **Curves by distance bin:** plot gain over persistence against steps, averaged within near, middle and far terciles, with a horizontal line at the target gain (LEEP Fig. 4). Keep the per-map raw curves in the appendix.
- **Several thresholds:** report cost at 25, 50 and 75% of the training-map gain, or a cost-vs-threshold curve, plus the area under the gain curve as a threshold-free measure (Taylor and Stone §2.1).
- **Threshold-free fit:** on the data-curve maps, fit gain(f) = E − G·f^−β per map (Mikami, Barnett) and correlate the fitted gap G with D.
- **Second outcome:** plot the training-map gain lost (forgetting) against D (WTE Fig. 3 bottom row; Westny Fig. 7). The design already measures it as a guard.
- **Normalize within target:** keep the persistence reference. Pooled correlations can vanish when target difficulty varies (Transferring GANs, my calculation above; yesterday's Blitzer and OTDD normalizations).
- **Distance variants side by side:** report ρ for the frozen symmetric D and for a directed coverage variant (Mensink Table 10; Westny Table 2 compares four measures in one table).

## 3. Verdict: partly

- **Done before:** a distance or score computed before training that predicts fine-tuned performance, and OT-family and sliced-OT distances used for it (s-OTDD, OTDD, WTE, OTCE). Theory that the target data needed depends on a divergence (Ben-David Theorem 3, Hanneke and Kpotufe).
- **Partly done:** distance against adaptation *speed* (LEEP Fig. 4, binned; Gao and Chaudhari's fine-tuning distance, with a distance that needs training) and learned-latent distances predicting fine-tuning and forgetting (Westny 2026).
- **New:** per-target adaptation *cost* curves for a generative world model, with a frozen pre-registered distance on the x axis, an adapter on frozen weights, a persistence-referenced outcome and motion weighting inside the distance. Nothing found does any two of these together.
- **Framing:** position the paper as "the transferability question asked of world models, with cost rather than accuracy as the outcome." Cite Hernandez (effective data), Ben-David Theorem 3 and Hanneke and Kpotufe as the theory. Do not claim that distance-predicts-transfer is new.

## Facts most likely to change the decision

1. Two studies find symmetric OT or Wasserstein distances predict transfer worse than directed coverage measures (Mensink Table 10: EMD τ = 0.20 vs target→nearest-source τ = 0.42; Westny Table 2: Wasserstein ρ ≈ 0.48 vs KL ≈ 0.81). Pre-register a directed variant as a secondary x axis before any adaptation result is read: the mean distance from each unseen-map latent to its nearest training-map latent, motion-weighted. It costs only a CPU pass over the frozen clouds.
2. Adaptation speed and final gain can respond to distance differently (Neyshabur §3.1). Far domains can also gain more (Gururangan §3.1). Report both cost-to-threshold and gain at 2,000 steps against D, rather than cost alone. Pick the threshold range with the pilot, before the full run (Taylor and Stone).
3. Knowledge-preserving adaptation underperforms plain fine-tuning on distant targets (AdAM §3). A far-map cost may reflect LoRA's capacity limit, not distance alone, so the single full fine-tune reference (Decision 5) belongs on a far map. The LLM finding that LoRA rank scaling is generally ineffective (Zhang et al. 2024) makes a flat rank ablation the likely outcome.
