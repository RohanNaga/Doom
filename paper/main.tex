\begin{abstract}
World models have become capable simulators, but they are evaluated almost exclusively on held-out rollouts from the domain they were trained on.
A small domain shift, such as a new map of the same game, lighting, textures or enemies, can make their outputs drift even when the underlying physics and game engine are unchanged.
With DoomShift, a controlled version of this shift in Doom, we measure what breaks and how much adaptation is needed to repair it.
We train three diffusion backbones, initialized from pretrained diffusion image models, on four Doom maps and evaluate them on 13 unseen maps.
All three keep their one-tic response to turning but fail to reproduce the new scene: scene PSNR drops by 2.8--3.3~dB and LPIPS rises by 0.126--0.145 across the three backbones.
This consistent gap shows that, one tic ahead, the response to turning transfers more reliably than scene appearance, independent of architecture or model scale.
Yet the lost appearance is relatively cheap to recover: rank-16 adapters trained on only eight episodes with 2\% of the original training updates recover 72--76\% of the LPIPS rise and about half of the lost PSNR.
Together, these results suggest that adapting a world model to a new map of the same game may mainly require restoring the scene's appearance.
\end{abstract}

\keywords{World Models, Diffusion Models, Few-shot Adaptation}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

World models learned from recorded play now render whole games in real time, predicting each update to the world from the player's actions in place of complex, hand-crafted game engines~\citep{valevski2024gamengen,alonso2024diamond,he2025matrixgame2}.
However, they are only scored against ground truth on held-out rollouts from their training environment~\citep{valevski2024gamengen,alonso2024diamond,po2026multigen}: none has been shown to generalize to a new environment in the same game.

A learned simulator is useful only where it is faithful and an agent or a robot moving to a new room, level or workspace needs to know how much of its world model still holds and the cost of fixing the rest.
Game worlds let us pose this question with the engine held fixed and only the environment changed: a new map of the same game preserves the physics and the controls exactly and only the layout, textures and lighting differ.
Yet unchanged dynamics alone do not guarantee a faithful render: pretrained video and world models are typically moved to a new domain only through a further full fine-tune~\citep{gao2025adaworld} or a dedicated adapter on the frozen backbone~\citep{rigter2024avid} and a model trained on a handful of maps tends to fall back on the appearance it already knows once the surroundings change.
Pretraining one model on every map a deployment might meet is never complete and training a separate model per map from scratch is prohibitively costly. We therefore ask what a world model keeps on a new map, what it loses and how few episodes and updates of adaptation recover the rest.

Our contributions (Figure~\ref{fig:teaser}):
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0pt}
\item We introduce DoomShift, a benchmark that isolates a scene shift: 17 Doom maps share the same physics and game engine and differ only in their surroundings, with four maps for training and 13 held out. We release the recordings, models, decoders and adapters.
\item We show that a scene shift breaks appearance far more than the response to turning: across three diffusion backbones, the models keep most of their one-tic turn response on unseen maps while their LPIPS roughly doubles and their scene PSNR drops 2.8--3.3~dB (Table~\ref{tab:unseen}).
\item We measure how much adaptation repairs the shift: a standard LoRA adapter trained on eight episodes of an unseen map recovers 72--76\% of the lost perceptual quality and roughly half of the lost PSNR, using only 2\% of the original training updates (Table~\ref{tab:unseen}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\textbf{Game world models.} Our work is most closely related to diffusion models that simulate a game from the player's actions: GameNGen runs Doom inside a diffusion model~\citep{valevski2024gamengen}, DIAMOND learns Atari and Counter-Strike in pixel space~\citep{alonso2024diamond}, Matrix-Game~2 scales the recipe to open worlds~\citep{he2025matrixgame2} and MultiGen trains a single Doom model across 100 procedurally generated maps~\citep{po2026multigen}. We build on GameNGen's backbone and training procedure and evaluate on maps the model never saw.

\textbf{Generalization of world models.} A world model can meet two kinds of shift: one that changes the dynamics, such as a new body or a new game, and a scene shift, in which the engine and its rules stay fixed and only the surroundings change. Most evaluations test the first kind: world models have been evaluated on a new robot body~\citep{chen2026xeworld} and on a whole new game~\citep{rigter2024avid} or simulator~\citep{gao2025adaworld}, where the dynamics change along with the scene. Scene shifts are rarer. For indoor navigation, Pathdreamer is evaluated in buildings not seen during training~\citep{koh2021pathdreamer}, the closest analogue to an unseen map, but its scenes are static and it does not study adaptation. Doom maps have been held out, but only to test the agents that play them~\citep{lample2017arnold,wydmuch2018vizdoom} and game world models that do show new scenes generate them from an image, with no recorded frames to score against~\citep{tong2026scope,bruce2024genie}. To our knowledge, no published game world model has been scored against recorded frames on held-out maps of its own game.

\textbf{Adaptation.} Pretrained video and world models are adapted to a new domain with a small adapter on a frozen backbone, as in AVID~\citep{rigter2024avid} and Vista~\citep{gao2024vista}, or with a short full fine-tune, as in AdaWorld~\citep{gao2025adaworld}. We use LoRA~\citep{hu2022lora}, which lowers the memory and compute adaptation needs. None of these works reports what adapting to one new scene costs in episodes and updates, which is what we measure.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:bench}

\textbf{Data.} We collect the data by running the Arnold agent~\citep{lample2017arnold} through ViZDoom~\citep{kempka2016vizdoom} deathmatches: free-for-all matches where each player scores by killing the others. Arnold ships with 17 Doom maps; each has its own layout, textures and lighting. For every episode, we let Arnold play against eight bots for up to 150 seconds. We record every tic, the engine's 1/35~s simulation step, capturing a 320$\times$240 frame and the 19 buttons the engine executed. Four maps (2 to 5) are our training maps: we train on 500 episodes of each and score on 25 further validation episodes per map (512 windows). The other thirteen maps (1, 6 to 17) are never trained on: we record 24 new episodes on each, using 16 for adaptation and holding out eight (256 windows) for scoring. In total we collect 2,412 episodes, about 96 hours of play, 12.1 million frames and 1.8~TB of recordings.

\textbf{Models and training.} We choose three pretrained backbones: the SD~1.4 U-Net~\citep{rombach2022ldm} (860M parameters) as the reference, following GameNGen~\citep{valevski2024gamengen}; PixArt-$\alpha$~\citep{chen2023pixart} (a 628M transformer in the same latent space) to see the effect of a different architecture; and SD~3.5 Medium~\citep{esser2024sd3} (a 2.27B MMDiT with 16-channel latents) to see the effect of a larger model.

We turn all three models into next-tic world models under one shared recipe (Figure~\ref{fig:method}). We give each model 32 context tics so it can see motion, train with $v$-prediction~\citep{salimans2022progressive}, minimizing $\mathrm{E}\,\|v_\theta(z_t, t, \mathbf{c}, a) - v\|^2$ with $v = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,\epsilon - \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,z_0$ for the next latent $z_0$, its noised version $z_t$, the 32 context latents $\mathbf{c}$ and the action $a$, for stable training across noise levels and add context noise augmentation as in GameNGen~\citep{valevski2024gamengen} so the model tolerates its own rollout errors. We train each backbone for 200k updates at batch size 32, keep an EMA at 0.9999 in fp32 and sample with 10-step DDIM~\citep{song2021ddim}, which we found to be a strong balance between quality and efficiency\apppar{app:protocol}.

\textbf{Metrics.} We score the part of each frame that shows the world (rows 0 to 207) and leave out the HUD rows below it, which do not change with the map.

For accuracy, we compute PSNR against the raw frame and for realism, LPIPS~\citep{zhang2018lpips}. The \emph{turn-response score} tests control. For each held-out window whose last action turns left or right (128 per map), we predict the next frame twice from the same context and noise, with the recorded turn and with it reversed, and measure each prediction's horizontal shift against the last context frame. The score is the fraction of windows whose shift changes sign, so ignoring the action scores 0. A perfect model scores about 0.89, since 11\% of recorded turns are too small to measure in one tic\apppar{app:protocol}.

\textbf{Decoder fine-tuning.} As in GameNGen~\citep{valevski2024gamengen}, we fine-tune each backbone's decoder on training-map frames with the encoder frozen, adding an LPIPS term to the loss (MSE plus 0.1~LPIPS); this happens once, before adaptation, the decoder stays frozen during it and it never sees an unseen map. This raises the scene reconstruction from 26.9 to 28.4~dB for the SD~1 decoder, which the U-Net and PixArt-$\alpha$ share and from 30.0 to 31.8~dB for the SD~3.5 decoder\apppar{app:protocol}.

\textbf{Adaptation.} For each unseen map we adapt each backbone with rank-16 LoRA~\citep{hu2022lora} on its attention layers~\citep{gao2024vista} and train its action and input embeddings in full~\citep{xie2023difffit}: 0.49, 4.1 and 1.8\% of the U-Net, PixArt-$\alpha$ and SD~3.5.
We train on eight episodes of the map and score on its eight held-out episodes at 0 to 8k updates. We found that updates, not data, limit adaptation: a single episode gave 75--101\% of the gain of sixteen\apppar{app:perarena-adapt}. We still use eight: that check covers only the U-Net on four maps.
We report 4k updates throughout, which give 96\% of the gain at 8k. At the pretraining batch size of 32 these are 2\% of the pretraining updates and, as each adapter update still runs a full forward and backward pass, roughly 2\% of its compute.
As a baseline, we fine-tune the whole U-Net on the same episodes.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\textbf{Zero-shot evaluation on unseen maps.}
On unseen maps, the models keep most of their turn response but fail to reproduce the new scene (Table~\ref{tab:unseen}).
Turn-response scores remain close to those on the training maps (0.79--0.81 vs.\ 0.84--0.86), while scene PSNR drops by 2.8--3.3~dB and LPIPS roughly doubles.
Every unseen map has worse LPIPS than every training map\apppar{app:distance}.
In the rollouts, moving forward, firing and turning all still produce the expected motion, but walls, floors and textures are often replaced by appearances learned from the training maps (Figure~\ref{fig:teaser}).
This holds across the U-Net, PixArt-$\alpha$ and SD~3.5, suggesting the failure is not specific to one architecture or scale.

\centering

\caption{\textbf{Zero-shot vs.\ adapted results per unseen map.} (a, b) Scene PSNR and LPIPS, zero-shot (open) and after 4k adaptation updates (filled). (c, d) Median statistics against adapter updates.}

\textbf{Adaptation cost.}
Adaptation primarily repairs the scene rather than the turn response (Table~\ref{tab:unseen}, Figure~\ref{fig:adapt}).
After 4k updates on eight episodes, the adapters recover 72--76\% of the LPIPS rise and about half of the lost PSNR (the easy group's 173\% comes from two maps that lost only 0.5~dB zero-shot), while turn-response scores rise only slightly, to 0.83--0.84.
The map's textures, colors and lighting return (Figure~\ref{fig:teaser}).

Most of this recovery happens early: half of the LPIPS rise is recovered within a median of 150--250 updates\apppar{app:perarena-adapt}, and training to 8k adds little.
Full U-Net fine-tuning recovers more of the LPIPS rise (88\% vs.\ 72\%), but requires 205$\times$ more trainable parameters and a 48 instead of a 16~GB GPU, and causes 2.7$\times$ more forgetting on the training maps (1.08 vs.\ 0.40~dB).
Thus, lightweight adaptation recovers most of the lost perceptual quality while leaving the turn response largely unchanged.
%===============================================================================

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:limits}

DoomShift shows that under a scene shift, world models keep most of their one-tic response to turning, and by eye to moving and firing, but lose the appearance of the new scene. Across three diffusion backbones, lightweight LoRA adaptation on only eight episodes restores much of this lost perceptual quality, while the turn response stays near its training-map level.

\textbf{Limitations.} We study one game, evaluate one tic ahead, and measure control only through turning actions; moving and firing are judged by eye.
\label{body-end}
