\begin{abstract}
World models have become capable simulators, but they are evaluated almost exclusively on held-out rollouts from the domain they were trained on.
A small domain shift, such as a new map of the same game, a new texture set, a lighting change or a different cast of enemies, makes their outputs drift and lose realism, though the underlying physics and game engine are exactly the same.
With DoomShift, a controlled version of this shift in Doom, we measure what it breaks and how much adaptation it takes to repair.
We train three diffusion backbones, initialized from pretrained diffusion image models, on four maps of Doom and score each on in-domain data and on 13 other out-of-distribution maps that it never saw.
All three keep their response to the controls and lose the scene: SD~3.5's perceptual error (LPIPS) doubles, from 0.126 to a median 0.262, and its scene PSNR drops 3.3~dB, from 25.4 to a median 22.1~dB; the SD~1.4 U-Net, GameNGen's backbone, and PixArt-$\alpha$ lose the same way.
Very little adaptation repairs it: rank-16 adapters trained on 8 episodes of each unseen map with 2\% of the original training updates recover 77\% of SD~3.5's lost perceptual quality and half of its lost PSNR, and 72\% and 76\% of the perceptual loss on the other two backbones.
\end{abstract}

\keywords{World models, Few-shot adaptation}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Game world models now render playable worlds from pixels and controls.
GameNGen runs Doom inside a diffusion model~\citep{valevski2024gamengen}, DIAMOND learns Atari and Counter-Strike in pixel space~\citep{alonso2024diamond} and Matrix-Game~2 scales the recipe to open worlds~\citep{he2025matrixgame2}.
Each of these models is evaluated only on held-out rollouts from the environment it was trained on, and none has been shown to generalize to a new environment within the same game.
This holds even for MultiGen, one Doom model trained across 100 procedurally generated maps~\citep{po2026multigen}: it reports PSNR, SSIM and LPIPS only on its training distribution, with no map held out.

A learned simulator is useful only where it is faithful, and a robot or an agent moving to a new room, level or workspace needs to know how much of its world model still holds there and what it costs to make the rest hold.
Game worlds let us pose this question with the engine held fixed: a new map of the same game preserves the physics and the controls exactly, and only the layout, textures and lighting differ.
Pretraining one model on every map a deployment might meet is never complete, and training a separate model per map from scratch is prohibitively costly. We therefore explore what it takes to close the domain gap that opens when a world model meets a shift in which the underlying physics and game engine are exactly the same and only the surroundings, scenario or scene change: what the model retains, what it loses, and how few episodes and updates recover the rest.

\caption{On an unseen map the U-Net still moves and fires but renders the training maps' masonry; eight adaptation episodes restore the map. Left: training maps (in distribution). Right: unseen map 7, zero-shot and after adaptation.}
\label{fig:teaser}

In short, a world model moved to a new map keeps playing the game but draws the wrong world, and a small adapter trained on eight episodes draws the map back (Figure~\ref{fig:teaser}). We make three contributions:
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0pt}
\item \textbf{DoomShift, a benchmark that isolates a scene shift.} We build the pipeline end to end (Figure~\ref{fig:method}): every-tic recordings, three diffusion world models initialized from pretrained image models and trained on four maps under one recipe, a fine-tuned decoder and a standard adapter, scored on the 13 maps the models never saw, zero-shot and after adaptation. Recordings, models, decoders and adapters are released (link withheld).
\item \textbf{A scene shift breaks appearance, not control.} On the unseen maps all three models keep their turn response (directional score 0.80 to 0.81, against 0.84 to 0.86 on the training maps) while their perceptual error roughly doubles (LPIPS 0.126 to a median 0.262 for SD~3.5) and their scene PSNR drops 2.8 to 3.3~dB. The loss is the same for a U-Net and two transformers from 0.6 to 2.3~B parameters, so across our three backbones it follows what the models were exposed to, not their architecture or scale. It is a step, not a slope: every unseen map is worse in LPIPS than every training map. And it lives in the model, since the decoder renders the unseen maps nearly as well as the training maps (Table~\ref{tab:unseen}, Figure~\ref{fig:teaser}).
\item \textbf{How much adaptation it takes: very little.} A standard LoRA adapter on the frozen model (0.49 percent of the U-Net's parameters), trained on eight episodes of the new map, recovers half of its lost perceptual quality in a median 250 updates. By 4k updates, 2 percent of pretraining and about one A4000 GPU-hour, it recovers 77 percent of SD~3.5's lost perceptual quality and half of its lost PSNR; unchanged, the same procedure recovers 72 and 76 percent on the U-Net and PixArt-$\alpha$. A full fine-tune with 205 times the trainable parameters recovers 87 percent against the adapter's 71 on four maps (Table~\ref{tab:unseen}, Figure~\ref{fig:adapt}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

Held-out evaluation of world models holds out something other than a map of the same game: a new robot body~\citep{chen2026xeworld}, a new game or environment~\citep{rigter2024avid,gao2025adaworld} or a new building~\citep{koh2021pathdreamer}. Doom maps have been held out, but only to test the agents that play them~\citep{lample2017arnold,wydmuch2018vizdoom}, and game world models that do show new scenes generate them from an image, with no recorded frames to score against~\citep{tong2026scope,bruce2024genie}. To our knowledge, no published game world model has been scored against recorded frames on maps of its own game that were deliberately held out from training. Our adapter is not new: a small adapter on a frozen video model moves it to a new setting in AVID~\citep{rigter2024avid} and adds action control in Vista~\citep{gao2024vista}, and AdaWorld adapts with a short full fine-tune~\citep{gao2025adaworld}, which we run as the comparator. None of them reports what adapting to one new scene costs in episodes and updates, which is what we measure.

\section{Study design}
\label{sec:bench}
\begin{figure}[b]
\centering
\figslot{figures/fig1_method_150.pdf}{\linewidth}{1.35in}{Figure 2: method overview}
\caption{What we built: (a) the benchmark, (b) three backbones under one recipe, (c) adaptation, (d) evaluation against the raw ground-truth frame.}
\label{fig:method}
\end{figure}


\textbf{Data.} We collect the data by running the Arnold agent~\citep{lample2017arnold} through ViZDoom~\citep{kempka2016vizdoom} deathmatches, free-for-all matches where each player scores by killing the others. Arnold ships with 17 Doom maps; each has its own layout, textures and lighting. In every episode Arnold plays 8 bots for 150 seconds, and we record every tic (1/35~s): a 320$\times$240 frame and the 19 buttons the engine executed. Four maps (2 to 5) are our training maps: we train on 500 episodes of each and score on 25 further validation episodes per map (512 windows). The other thirteen (1, 6 to 17) are never trained on: 24 new episodes each, 16 for adaptation and 8 held out for scoring (256 windows); 2,412 episodes, 96 hours of play and 12.1 million frames in all.

\textbf{Three backbones, one training recipe.} Our anchor is the SD~1.4 U-Net~\citep{rombach2022ldm} (860M parameters): it is GameNGen's backbone~\citep{valevski2024gamengen}, so our in-distribution numbers come from the same network as the published Doom result. To change the architecture at fixed scale we swap in PixArt-$\alpha$~\citep{chen2023pixart}, a 628M transformer in the same latent space; to change the scale we use SD~3.5 Medium~\citep{esser2024sd3}, a 2.27B MMDiT whose 16-channel latents give its decoder a higher reconstruction score (31.8 against 28.6~dB on the training maps).

We turn all three into next-tic world models under one shared recipe (Figure~\ref{fig:method}). Each model gets 32 context tics so it can see motion, $v$-prediction~\citep{salimans2022progressive} and context noise augmentation as in GameNGen~\citep{valevski2024gamengen} so it tolerates its own rollout errors; we train each for 200k updates at batch size 32 with an fp32 EMA at 0.9999 and sample with 10-step DDIM~\citep{song2021ddim}, which we found to be a strong balance between quality and efficiency (sampler sweep in \appref{app:protocol}).

\textbf{Metrics.} We score the part of each frame that shows the world (rows 0 to 207) and leave out the HUD rows below it, which do not change with the map.

For accuracy, we compute PSNR against the raw frame, and for realism, LPIPS~\citep{zhang2018lpips}. Absolute PSNR alone does not compare maps, because it rises with how static a recording is: copying the last context frame forward, the persistence baseline of video prediction~\citep{mathieu2016deep}, already scores 19.8~dB on the unseen maps. For turning, we compute the \emph{directional} score: the fraction of turning windows whose predicted view turns the other way once we swap left and right in the newest control.


\textbf{Decoder.} Every backbone is trained on latents from its frozen encoder, so the encoder stays fixed: changing it would move every training target. The decoder only renders, so fine-tuning it changes what we can see, not what the model knows.
We fine-tune the SD~1 decoder $D$ on training-map frames paired with their frozen-encoder latents, using MSE plus 0.1~LPIPS (\appref{app:protocol}); it never sees an unseen map.
On 200 validation frames it raises the scene reconstruction from 26.9 to 28.4~dB and lowers its LPIPS from 0.092 to 0.060; the LPIPS term is there because MSE alone more than triples scene LPIPS (0.092 to 0.339).
Every pixel score below uses it, and SD~3.5 its own decoder tuned the same way.

\textbf{Adaptation as a measurement.} To measure how much adaptation a map needs, we keep the trained model frozen and train a standard adapter on a few episodes of that map, then score the adapted model on held-out episodes at each update budget.
For each unseen map we adapt each backbone's 200k EMA weights with rank-16 LoRA~\citep{hu2022lora} on every attention projection of the frozen backbone~\citep{gao2024vista} and train the control MLP, input projection and noise-bucket embedding in full (4.2M parameters, 0.49 percent of the U-Net; the transformers' counts in \appref{app:perarena-adapt})~\citep{xie2023difffit}.
We train on 8 adaptation episodes (for the U-Net about 1 A4000 GPU-hour per map for 4k updates) and score the non-EMA weights at 0, 50, 100, 150, 250, 500, 1k, 2k, 4k and 8k updates, with the control checks (forgetting on the training maps and the directional check) at 0, 4k and 8k. 4k updates is the elbow of the curve, and we report it everywhere: across the 13 unseen maps it captures most of the benefit (on average 96 percent of each map's 8k gain; Figure~\ref{fig:adapt}) at the smallest budget; 8k serves as the check. As a comparator we fine-tune all 860M parameters of the U-Net on the same eight episodes.
A map's budget is the first grid point at which it recovers at least half of its zero-shot LPIPS rise over the training maps; maps that do not by 8k are right-censored~\citep{taylor2009transfer}.

\textbf{The shift breaks the scene, not the controls.} On unseen maps the turn response survives (directional 0.80 to 0.81 against 0.84 to 0.86 in distribution; Table~\ref{tab:unseen}).
The appearance does not: the U-Net's scene PSNR falls from 25.2~dB on the training maps to a median 22.3~dB and its LPIPS rises from 0.158 to 0.303 (per map in Figure~\ref{fig:adapt}).
Pooled over the 13 unseen maps, one predicted frame in five has an LPIPS above 0.4, against one in 73 on the training maps; such frames concentrate in maps 16, 17, 13 and 1 (32 to 59 percent of their windows), while seven maps stay below 10 percent.
The decoder is not the limit: it reconstructs the unseen maps nearly as well as the training maps (median 27.3 against 28.6~dB, LPIPS 0.05 to 0.09).
The three backbones lose the same things (Table~\ref{tab:unseen}; \appref{app:distance}): scene PSNR drops 2.9, 2.8 and 3.3~dB and LPIPS rises by 0.145, 0.126 and 0.136, so across our three backbones the shift looks like a property of the task, not of the architecture or the scale.
It is a step, not a slope: every unseen map has a worse LPIPS than every training map for all three backbones (\appref{app:distance}).
What the model keeps is how actions act on the world: zero-shot on map 7 the weapon fires and the view advances as in the ground truth (Figure~\ref{fig:teaser}); the loss sits in the scene, whose rows fall 2.9~dB while the map-independent HUD rows fall 0.2~dB (29.7 to 29.5).

\textbf{How much adaptation: updates, episodes, parameters.}
Eight episodes and 4k updates raise the median scene PSNR from 22.3 to 23.6~dB and lower the median LPIPS from 0.303 to 0.210 (Figure~\ref{fig:adapt}, Table~\ref{tab:unseen}); the 8k check adds little (23.7~dB, 0.204).
The median map's budget is 250 updates (100 to 8k per map, none censored; 12 of the 13 cross by 4k), and half of each map's 8k gain arrives within 100 updates on average.
By the same 4k updates it recovers a median 72 percent of each map's rise in LPIPS over the training maps (at least 48 percent) and a median 48 percent of its lost scene PSNR (over 100 percent on maps 8, 9 and 10, whose static recordings already sit near the training maps' PSNR).
About a decibel of the PSNR still missing against the training maps is the decoder's own reconstruction drop on the unseen maps (27.3 against 28.6~dB), which no adapter of the world model can recover.
The procedure carries over unchanged to the other backbones.
On PixArt-$\alpha$ the adapter raises the median scene PSNR on the 13 unseen maps from 22.4 to 23.7~dB and lowers LPIPS from 0.285 to 0.205 by 4k: it recovers a median 76 percent of the rise in LPIPS and 48 percent of the lost PSNR with a median budget of 250 updates.
On SD~3.5 the same adapter raises the median scene PSNR on the 13 unseen maps from 22.1 to 24.0~dB and lowers LPIPS from 0.262 to 0.165 by 4k: it recovers a median 77 percent of the rise in LPIPS over its in-distribution 0.126 (at least 54 percent) and 50 percent of the lost PSNR.
Its median budget is also 250 updates.
A full fine-tune of all 860M parameters on the same eight episodes does better on each of the four comparator maps 6, 7, 8 and 16: at 4k it reaches a median scene PSNR of 23.1~dB and LPIPS of 0.182 against the adapter's 22.9~dB and 0.209 (0.20 to 0.29~dB and 0.017 to 0.030 in LPIPS per map) and recovers a median 87 percent of the rise in LPIPS against the adapter's 71. It trains 205 times as many parameters and needs a 49~GB A6000 (0.8 hours per map) where the adapter fits a 16~GB A4000 (1.1 hours).
Data matters less than the first updates: on the four maps of the data ladder, one episode already gives most of the gain of sixteen (\appref{app:perarena-adapt}).
The adapters trade some in-distribution skill for it: the training maps' scene PSNR falls at the 8k check on every map (median 0.59~dB) while the directional score rises from 0.81 to 0.84.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:limits}

The claims cover 13 unseen maps of one game, one agent, one-tic scoring and mostly one seed.
One-tic quality does not guarantee stable rollouts (\appref{app:closedloop}).
Next are other Doom maps and other games, to see whether the same procedure transfers; detecting a shift from the model's own zero-shot scores so that it repairs itself; closed-loop evaluation of the adapted models; and agents trained inside the adapted model.
The pattern we measure, control kept, appearance lost, most of it relearned from a few episodes, is what a deployed world model would need to detect and repair on its own, and DoomShift is a testbed for that loop.
\label{body-end}
