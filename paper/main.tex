\begin{abstract}
World models have become capable simulators, but they are tested primarily in the domain they were trained on.
A small domain shift, a new arena of the same game, makes their outputs drift and lose realism, though the physics is unchanged and only the situation differs.
DoomShift asks how quickly and cheaply a world model can be adapted across such a shift: one Doom level pack has 17 arenas; we train three pretrained diffusion backbones on four and score each of the other thirteen against persistence (the last frame copied forward), zero-shot for all three and after adaptation for the U-Net.
Off the training maps the U-Net still beats persistence on every arena, but its advantage shrinks from 5.1~dB in distribution to 1.1--2.9~dB (median 2.0) and its LPIPS is worse than persistence's on 13 of 13, while its turn response survives.
Rank-16 adapters trained on eight episodes close half of an arena's gap to the in-distribution advantage within 8k updates on 11 of 13 arenas (9 within 4k), recover a median 1.8~dB and beat persistence perceptually on 11 of 13 at 4k.
\end{abstract}

\keywords{World models, Domain shift, Few-shot adaptation}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Game world models now render playable worlds from pixels and controls: GameNGen runs Doom inside a diffusion model~\citep{valevski2024gamengen}, DIAMOND learns Atari and Counter-Strike in pixel space~\citep{alonso2024diamond}, and Matrix-Game~2 scales the recipe to open worlds~\citep{he2025matrixgame2}.
MultiGen trains one Doom model on 100 procedurally generated maps with a pre-trained agent~\citep{po2026multigen}, the closest prior work to ours; it scores PSNR, SSIM and LPIPS on its training distribution and reports no map outside it.
Each operates in the one environment it was trained on and is judged only there: its score is a held-out trajectory of its training scenes, and none is shown to generalize to a new environment of the same game.

A learned simulator is only useful where it is faithful, and a robot or an agent that moves to a new room, level or workspace needs to know how much of its world model still holds there and what it costs to make the rest hold.
Game worlds let us ask that question with the engine held fixed and only the environment changed.
A new arena keeps the physics and the controls but changes the layout, textures and lighting, and a model trained on a few maps renders it in the appearance it knows; training a model per map costs too much, so the question is how cheaply post-training closes the gap.
We ask: when a world model meets a new arena of the same game, what does it keep, what does it lose, and how many episodes and updates does it take to relearn the rest?

This paper turns that question into a benchmark: 17 arenas of one Doom level pack, three pretrained diffusion backbones trained on four of them, and the other thirteen scored zero-shot and after post-training.
We contribute:
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0pt}
\item \textbf{DoomShift}, the benchmark and its dataset: every-tic recordings of \prov{6,000} episodes on the four training maps and 24 on each of the 13 unseen arenas (this paper trains on 2,000 of them), with the three trained models, the decoder fine-tune and the adapter path released (Figure~\ref{fig:method}; link withheld for review).
\item A post-training recipe that adapts a frozen model to a new arena from eight episodes with a small adapter, and the per-arena protocol that scores the budget it needs.
\item The finding that it generalizes without heavy retraining: half of the gap to the training maps closed on 11 of 13 unseen arenas within 8k updates (9 within 4k), most of the gain within 250, and a perceptual advantage over persistence (the last frame copied forward) restored on 11 of 13 at 4k (Figure~\ref{fig:adapt}; full fine-tune comparator \tbd{}).
\item The measured shift and what predicts it: on unseen arenas the PSNR advantage over persistence shrinks and the perceptual margin changes sign for all three backbones while the turn response survives (Figures~\ref{fig:teaser} and~\ref{fig:shift}); the model's own zero-shot skill score is the best predictor we found of where an arena ends up, while the registered distances between recordings order nothing.
\end{itemize}

\section{Prior work}
\label{sec:related}

Held-out evaluation of world models has meant a new embodiment (concurrent work~\citep{chen2026xeworld}), a new game or environment~\citep{rigter2024avid,gao2025adaworld} or a new building~\citep{koh2021pathdreamer}; to our knowledge, no published game world model is scored on unseen maps of the same game, and Doom map holdouts exist only for agents~\citep{lample2017arnold,wydmuch2018vizdoom}.
Pretrained video models have been moved to new domains by a short full fine-tune (AdaWorld~\citep{gao2025adaworld}) or by a small adapter on a frozen model (AVID~\citep{rigter2024avid}); Vista adds action control to a frozen backbone with LoRA~\citep{gao2024vista}.
We measure the budget a small adapter needs per scene.
Persistence is the standard reference of video prediction~\citep{mathieu2016deep,lotter2017prednet,villegas2019fidelity}; game world models dropped it, and absolute PSNR then ranks how static a recording is.
The reconstruction upper bound, the decoder applied to the ground-truth latent, is in practice the best a latent model rendered through that decoder can score~\citep{zheng2023occworld,karypidis2024dinoforesight}, and we read every score beside it.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:bench}

\textbf{Data.} Every episode is a ViZDoom~\citep{kempka2016vizdoom} deathmatch, a free-for-all match in which each player scores by killing the others, on one of the 17 arenas of the Arnold agent's level pack~\citep{lample2017arnold}.
A level pack is a Doom WAD file that bundles a set of maps, and an arena is one of its maps, with its own layout, textures and lighting.
Arnold plays against 8 bots for 150~s, recorded at every tic, the engine's 1/35~s simulation step, as 320$\times$240 frames with the 19 executed buttons.
Of the 17 arenas, four (2 to 5, 500 episodes each) are the training maps, scored on 25 validation episodes each (512 windows); the other thirteen (1, 6 to 17), never trained on, each have 24 new episodes, 16 for adaptation and 8 held out (256 windows); in total 2,412 episodes, about 96 hours of play and about 12.1 million frames.

\textbf{Three backbones, one training recipe.} The SD~1.4 U-Net~\citep{rombach2022ldm} (860M parameters) is the anchor: it is GameNGen's backbone~\citep{valevski2024gamengen}, so our in-distribution numbers come from the same network as the published Doom result.
PixArt-$\alpha$~\citep{chen2023pixart} asks the architecture question at fixed scale, a 628M transformer instead of a U-Net in the same latent space, and architecture does not change what survives the shift (in distribution their PSNR and $A$ differ by under 0.1~dB; on unseen arenas their median $A$ falls to 2.03 and 2.19~dB and both lose to persistence in LPIPS on all 13; Table~\ref{tab:unseen}).
SD~3.5 Medium~\citep{esser2024sd3}, a widely adopted open MMDiT (2.27B parameters, 16-channel latents), asks the scale question; its latent space changes with it, so that step compares pretrained packages, not architectures.
All three become next-tic world models under one recipe (Figure~\ref{fig:method}): 32 context tics so the model sees motion, $v$-prediction~\citep{salimans2022progressive} for stable training across noise levels, and context noise augmentation as in GameNGen~\citep{valevski2024gamengen} so the model tolerates its own rollout errors.
Each trains for 200k updates of batch 32, a step count matched across backbones, keeps an EMA at 0.9999 in fp32 because a bf16 EMA underflows and freezes, and is sampled with 10-step DDIM~\citep{song2021ddim} so that evaluation stays cheap (\appref{app:protocol}).

\textbf{What we measure.} Four scores answer four questions about one predicted frame $\hat z$, given the ground-truth next frame $x$ with latent $z$, the last context frame $x_\text{last}$ with latent $z_\text{last}$, and the decoder $D$.
How much better than copying is it? The \emph{decoded advantage} $A=\mathrm{PSNR}(D(\hat z),D(z))-\mathrm{PSNR}(D(z_\text{last}),D(z))$, a paired difference in the spirit of Genie's $\Delta$PSNR~\citep{bruce2024genie}, with persistence rather than random actions as the reference.
Does it look better than copying? The \emph{perceptual margin} $M=\mathrm{LPIPS}(D(\hat z),x)-\mathrm{LPIPS}(x_\text{last},x)$~\citep{zhang2018lpips}, negative when it does.
How far is it from the best the decoder can render? The \emph{gap} $G=\mathrm{PSNR}(D(z),x)-\mathrm{PSNR}(D(\hat z),x)$ to the reconstruction upper bound.
Does it turn the right way? The \emph{directional} score, the fraction of turning windows whose predicted view turns the other way when left and right are swapped in the newest control.
Before any decoding, the \emph{zero-shot latent skill score} $S_0$, $10\log_{10}$ of persistence's latent MSE over the model's, scores the model in its own space.
$M$ and $G$ are scored against the raw frame; pixel scores use the scene rows 0 to 207, since persistence copies the HUD almost exactly.
$A_\text{train}$, the advantage on the training maps (5.06~dB, episode-bootstrap interval 4.75 to 5.38), is the in-distribution reference every arena is measured against, not an arena's own upper bound.

\textbf{Training setup.} Each backbone trains on one RTX A6000 (49~GB) in bf16 with fused AdamW, at 1.8 updates per second for the U-Net, 1.4 for PixArt-$\alpha$ and 0.53 for SD~3.5; each adapter trains on one RTX A4000 (16~GB) at about 1.0 updates per second.

\textbf{Decoder.} Every backbone is trained on latents from its frozen encoder, so the latent space is the contract between encoder, world model and decoder: changing the encoder would move every training target and invalidate the trained backbones, the adapters and the reconstruction upper bound.
The decoder only renders: it sits after the model, and in $A$ its errors are shared by the prediction, the rendered ground truth and persistence, so fine-tuning it changes what we can see, not what the model knows.
We fine-tune the SD~1 decoder $D$ on training-map frames paired with their frozen-encoder latents, using MSE plus 0.1~LPIPS for 3,486 updates and gating it on held-out training-map frames (\appref{app:protocol}); it never sees an unseen arena.
It raises the scene reconstruction from 26.9 to 28.4~dB and lowers its LPIPS from 0.092 to 0.060, yet moves $A$ by only 0.2~dB zero-shot and 0.7~dB after adaptation, so the shift lives in the model, not the renderer; the LPIPS term is there because MSE alone more than triples scene LPIPS (0.092 to 0.339).
We use it for every pixel score of the U-Net and PixArt-$\alpha$ unless stated otherwise; SD~3.5 uses its own stock decoder.

\textbf{In-distribution performance.} On the training maps all three backbones beat persistence one tic ahead in PSNR and LPIPS and turn the right way ($A$ from \prov{+3.89} to +5.06~dB, directional 0.84 to 0.86 against 0.885 for the ground-truth frames; Table~\ref{tab:unseen}).
Four tics ahead they reach 21.33, 21.29 and \prov{21.59}~dB against persistence's 19.21~dB (stock decoders, full frame; SD~3.5 at 140k).

\textbf{Post-training recipe.} To move a trained model to a new arena we keep its weights frozen and train a small adapter on a few episodes of that arena, then score the adapted model on held-out episodes at each update budget.
For each unseen arena we adapt the U-Net 200k EMA weights with rank-16 LoRA~\citep{hu2022lora} on every attention projection of the frozen backbone~\citep{gao2024vista} and train the control MLP, input projection and noise-bucket embedding in full (4.2M parameters, 0.49 percent)~\citep{xie2023difffit}.
We train on 8 adaptation episodes (1.1 A4000 GPU-hours per arena for 4k updates) and score the non-EMA weights at 0, 50, 100, 150, 250, 500, 1k, 2k, 4k and 8k updates, with the control checks (forgetting, $A$ on the training maps, and the directional check) at 0 and 8k; a full fine-tune comparator at $2\times10^{-5}$ is in progress (\tbd{}).
An arena's budget is the first grid point at which $A$ reaches $\tfrac12(A_0+A_\text{train})$, half of its gap from its zero-shot $A_0$; arenas that do not by the final budget of the grid are right-censored~\citep{taylor2009transfer}.
Half of the gap in dB is not half of the error: the median gain of 1.8~dB removes 34 percent of the model's starting squared error (geometric mean over windows).

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

We report what a model loses zero-shot, what post-training recovers and with what budget, and what predicts how far an arena gets.

\textbf{Zero-shot: where the deficit sits.} What does a model keep and what does it lose when it meets an arena it never saw? We answer with the four scores of Section~\ref{sec:bench}. On unseen arenas the turn response survives (U-Net reversal rates 0.73 to 0.86 per arena against 0.79 to 0.91 in distribution; Table~\ref{tab:unseen}).
$A$ stays positive on every arena and backbone but falls from 5.06 to a median 2.03~dB, while the decoder's scene reconstruction PSNR is 25.2 to 29.7~dB and $G$ grows from 3.4 to a median 5.0~dB.
Nor is the perceptual sign reversal the decoder's floor: its own reconstruction LPIPS (0.05 to 0.09) is far below persistence's (0.18 to 0.28), yet the prediction's LPIPS exceeds it on 13 of 13 arenas ($M$ +0.03 to +0.17, median +0.08, against $-$0.08 in distribution; Figure~\ref{fig:shift}b).
The three backbones lose the same things (Table~\ref{tab:unseen}, Figure~\ref{fig:shift}a): the shift looks like a property of the task, not of the architecture or the scale.
What the model keeps is how actions act on the world: zero-shot on arena 7 the weapon fires and the view advances as in the ground truth (Figure~\ref{fig:teaser}) and the turn response holds; the loss sits mostly in the scene, whose rows fall from 27.5 to a median 24.0~dB on unseen arenas while the map-independent HUD rows move from 35.5 to 33.8~dB (U-Net, PSNR against the rendered ground truth, as in $A$).

\textbf{Post-training: what it takes.} We next adapt a small subset of the U-Net's parameters on each unseen arena and ask what it takes to recover what was lost.
Eight episodes and 8k updates raise the median $A$ from 2.04 to 3.94~dB, and on average 66 percent of each arena's gain comes within 250 updates; the last 4k updates add 0.10~dB, on every arena (Figure~\ref{fig:adapt}, Table~\ref{tab:cost}).
11 of 13 arenas close half of their gap (9 by 4k; arenas 1 and 8 cross at the 8k read by under 0.05~dB), with a median budget of 1k updates [250, 8k] and 5 arenas within 500; one reaches $A_\text{train}$.
Data matters less than the first updates: on the four arenas of the data ladder, one episode already gives most of the gain of sixteen (\appref{app:perarena-adapt}).
At 4k, the last budget scored against the raw frame, the prediction's LPIPS is below persistence's on 11 of 13 arenas, the other two within 0.01, and the median $G$ falls from 5.0 to the in-distribution 3.4~dB.
The adapters trade some in-distribution skill for it: $A$ on the training maps falls on every arena (median $-0.59$~dB, $-0.28$ to $-1.65$; stock decoder), while the directional score rises from 0.81 to 0.84.

\textbf{What predicts the outcome.} The frozen model's zero-shot skill score $S_0$ tracks where an arena ends up, not how much it gains (exploratory Spearman $+0.88$ with $A$ at 8k, $+0.25$ with the gain, $-0.59$ with the budget); a frame distance does not. Before scoring any map we froze $d$, the sliced Wasserstein-2 distance between the motion-weighted cloud of a map's per-frame SD~1 latents and that of its nearest training map (\appref{app:distance}).
It places the training maps' held-out episodes inside the train-versus-train floor (0.03 to 0.07 against 0.02 to 0.09) and every unseen arena outside it (0.12 to 0.27), but within the 13 arenas it orders nothing (Spearman $-0.22$ with $A_0$, $-0.33$ with the budget); two registered transition-level distances fail even the separation (\appref{app:distance}).

\section{Future work}
\label{sec:limits}

On unseen arenas a Doom world model keeps its turn response and its action effects and loses the arena's appearance; eight episodes and at most 8k adapter updates close half of its deficit on 11 of 13 arenas, most of each arena's gain arriving in the first 250, and restore a perceptual advantage over persistence on 11 of 13.
The claims cover 13 arenas of one level pack, one agent, one-tic scoring and mostly one seed, and persistence is a weak reference where the camera moves fast.
One-tic quality does not guarantee stable rollouts: SD~3.5, best at one tic on the full frame with stock decoders, collapses in 23 non-EMA and 9 EMA rollouts of 256, and the EMA removes checkpoint-specific collapses, not the failure mode (\appref{app:closedloop}).
Next are other level packs and other games, to see whether the same recipe transfers; detecting a shift from the model's own zero-shot skill score so that it repairs itself; closed-loop evaluation of the adapted models; and agents trained inside the adapted model.
The pattern we measure, control kept, appearance lost, most of it relearned from a few episodes, is what a deployed world model would need to detect and repair on its own, and DoomShift is a testbed for that loop.
\label{body-end}
