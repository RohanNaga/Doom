\begin{abstract}
World models have become capable simulators, but they are evaluated almost exclusively on held-out rollouts from the domain they were trained on.
A small domain shift, such as a new map of the same game, a new texture set, a lighting change or a different cast of enemies, makes their outputs drift and lose realism, though the underlying physics and game engine are exactly the same.
With DoomShift, a controlled version of this domain shift in Doom, we measure what it breaks and how much adaptation it takes to repair.
We train three diffusion backbones, initialized from pretrained diffusion image models, on four maps of Doom and score each on in-distribution data and on 13 out-of-distribution maps that it never saw.
All three keep their response to controls and lose the scene: SD~3.5's perceptual error (LPIPS) doubles, from 0.126 to a median 0.262, and its scene PSNR drops 3.3~dB, from 25.4 to a median 22.1~dB; the SD~1.4 U-Net, GameNGen's backbone, and PixArt-$\alpha$ lose the same way.
Very little adaptation repairs it: rank-16 adapters trained on 8 episodes of each unseen map with 2\% of the original training updates recover 77\% of SD~3.5's lost perceptual quality and half of its lost PSNR, and 72\% and 76\% of the perceptual loss on the other two backbones.
\end{abstract}

\keywords{World models, Domain shift, Few-shot adaptation}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

World models learned from recorded play now render whole games in real time, predicting each update to the environment from the player's actions in place of a complex, manually crafted game engine~\citep{valevski2024gamengen,alonso2024diamond,he2025matrixgame2}.
However, these models are still evaluated only on held-out rollouts from the environment they were trained on~\citep{valevski2024gamengen,alonso2024diamond,he2025matrixgame2,po2026multigen}: none has been shown to generalize to a new environment within the same game.

A learned simulator is useful only where it is faithful, and an agent or a robot moving to a new room, level or workspace needs to know how much of its world model still holds there and what it costs to make the rest hold.
Game worlds let us pose this question with the engine held fixed and only the environment changed: a new map of the same game preserves the physics and the controls exactly, and only the layout, textures and lighting differ.
Yet unchanged dynamics alone do not guarantee a faithful render: pretrained video and world models are typically moved to a new domain only through a further full fine-tune~\citep{gao2025adaworld} or a dedicated adapter on the frozen backbone~\citep{rigter2024avid}, and a model trained on a handful of maps tends to fall back on the appearance it already knows once the surroundings change.
Pretraining one model on every map a deployment might meet is never complete, and training a separate model per map from scratch is prohibitively costly. We therefore explore what it takes to close the domain gap that opens when a world model meets a shift in which the underlying physics and game engine are exactly the same and only the surroundings, scenario or scene change: what the model retains, what it loses, and how few episodes and updates recover the rest.

In short, a world model moved to a new map keeps playing the game but draws the wrong world, and a small adapter trained on eight episodes draws the map back (Figure~\ref{fig:teaser}). Our contributions:
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0pt}
\item We introduce DoomShift, a benchmark that isolates a scene shift: 17 Doom maps share the same physics and game engine and differ only in their surroundings, with four maps for training and 13 held out. We release the recordings, models, decoders and adapters.
\item We show that a scene shift breaks appearance, not control: across three diffusion backbones, the models keep their response to controls on the unseen maps while their perceptual error (LPIPS) roughly doubles and their scene PSNR drops 2.8 to 3.3~dB.
\item We measure how much adaptation repairs the shift: a standard LoRA adapter trained on eight episodes of an unseen map recovers 72 to 77 percent of the lost perceptual quality and roughly half of the lost PSNR, using only 2 percent of the original training updates.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\textbf{Game world models.} Our work is most closely related to diffusion models that simulate a game from the player's actions: GameNGen runs Doom inside a diffusion model~\citep{valevski2024gamengen}, DIAMOND learns Atari and Counter-Strike in pixel space~\citep{alonso2024diamond}, Matrix-Game~2 scales the recipe to open worlds~\citep{he2025matrixgame2} and MultiGen trains a single Doom model across 100 procedurally generated maps~\citep{po2026multigen}. All four report metrics only on the environments they were trained on. We build on GameNGen's backbone and training procedure and evaluate on maps the model never saw.

\textbf{Generalization of world models.} World models have been evaluated on a new robot body~\citep{chen2026xeworld} and on a whole new game~\citep{rigter2024avid} or simulator~\citep{gao2025adaworld}, where the dynamics change along with the scene. Closest to an unseen map, Pathdreamer predicts views inside buildings not seen in training~\citep{koh2021pathdreamer}, for navigation through static scans and without adaptation. Doom maps have been held out, but only to test the agents that play them~\citep{lample2017arnold,wydmuch2018vizdoom}, and game world models that do show new scenes generate them from an image, with no recorded frames to score against~\citep{tong2026scope,bruce2024genie}. To our knowledge, no published game world model has been scored against recorded frames on maps of its own game that were deliberately held out from training.

\textbf{Adaptation.} Our adapter is not new: a small adapter on a frozen video model moves it to a new setting in AVID~\citep{rigter2024avid} and adds action control in Vista~\citep{gao2024vista}, and AdaWorld adapts with a short full fine-tune~\citep{gao2025adaworld}, which we run as the baseline. None of them reports what adapting to one new scene costs in episodes and updates, which is what we measure.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:bench}

\textbf{Data.} We collect the data for this research by running experiments with the Arnold agent~\citep{lample2017arnold} through ViZDoom~\citep{kempka2016vizdoom} deathmatches: free-for-all matches where each player scores by killing the others. Arnold ships with 17 Doom maps; each has its own layout, textures and lighting. For every episode, we let Arnold play against 8 bots for 150 seconds. We record every tic, the engine's 1/35~s simulation step, capturing a 320$\times$240 frame and the 19 buttons the engine executed. Four maps (2 to 5) are our training maps: we train on 500 episodes of each and score on 25 further validation episodes per map (512 windows). The other thirteen maps (1, 6 to 17) are never trained on: we record 24 new episodes on each, using 16 for adaptation and holding out 8 (256 windows) for scoring. In total we collect 2,412 episodes, about 96 hours of play and about 12.1 million frames.

\textbf{Models and training.} We pick three pretrained backbones to ask three different questions. Our anchor is the SD~1.4 U-Net~\citep{rombach2022ldm} (860M parameters): it is GameNGen's backbone~\citep{valevski2024gamengen}, so our in-distribution numbers come from the same network as the published Doom result. To test how different architectures perform at fixed scale, we swap in PixArt-$\alpha$~\citep{chen2023pixart}, a 628M transformer in the same latent space instead of a U-Net. To test how performance changes with scale, we use SD~3.5 Medium~\citep{esser2024sd3}, a widely adopted open MMDiT (2.27B parameters, 16-channel latents). Its 16-channel latents give its decoder a higher reconstruction score (31.8 against 28.6~dB on the training maps).

We turn all three into next-tic world models under one shared recipe (Figure~\ref{fig:method}). We give each model 32 context tics so it can see motion, train with $v$-prediction~\citep{salimans2022progressive} for stable training across noise levels and add context noise augmentation as in GameNGen~\citep{valevski2024gamengen} so the model tolerates its own rollout errors. We train each backbone for 200k updates at batch size 32, keep an EMA at 0.9999 in fp32 and sample with 10-step DDIM~\citep{song2021ddim}, which we found to be a strong balance between quality and efficiency (sampler sweep in \appref{app:protocol}).

\textbf{Metrics.} We score the part of each frame that shows the world (rows 0 to 207) and leave out the HUD rows below it, which do not change with the map.

For accuracy, we compute PSNR against the raw frame, and for realism, LPIPS~\citep{zhang2018lpips}. Absolute PSNR alone does not compare maps, because it rises with how static a recording is: copying the last context frame forward, the persistence baseline of video prediction~\citep{mathieu2016deep}, already scores 19.8~dB on the unseen maps (18.5 to 22.5 per map). For turning, we compute the \emph{directional} score: we predict each turning window twice, once with the recorded control and once with left and right swapped in the newest control, and report the fraction of windows whose predicted view turns the other way. A model that ignores the control scores 0, and the ground-truth frames turn the way their control says in 0.89 of the windows, which is the reference for the score (\appref{app:protocol}).

\textbf{Decoder fine-tuning.} Every backbone is trained on latents from its frozen encoder, so the latent space is the contract between encoder, world model and decoder: changing the encoder would move every training target and invalidate the trained backbones, the adapters and the decoder's reconstruction scores.
The decoder only renders: it sits after the model, and its own error is what its reconstruction score measures, so fine-tuning it changes what we can see, not what the model knows.
We fine-tune the SD~1 decoder $D$ on training-map frames paired with their frozen-encoder latents, using MSE plus 0.1~LPIPS for 3,486 updates and gating it on held-out training-map frames (\appref{app:protocol}); it never sees an unseen map.
On 200 validation frames it raises the scene reconstruction from 26.9 to 28.4~dB and lowers its LPIPS from 0.092 to 0.060; the LPIPS term is there because MSE alone more than triples scene LPIPS (0.092 to 0.339).
We use it for every pixel score of the U-Net and PixArt-$\alpha$ unless stated otherwise; SD~3.5 uses its own decoder, fine-tuned the same way.

\textbf{Adaptation.} To measure how much adaptation a map needs, we keep the trained model frozen and train a standard adapter on a few episodes of that map, then score the adapted model on held-out episodes at each update budget.
For each unseen map we adapt each backbone's 200k EMA weights with rank-16 LoRA~\citep{hu2022lora} on every attention projection of the frozen backbone~\citep{gao2024vista} and train the control MLP, input projection and noise-bucket embedding in full (4.2M parameters, 0.49 percent of the U-Net; 25.9M, 4.1 percent, of PixArt-$\alpha$; 40.6M, 1.8 percent, of SD~3.5)~\citep{xie2023difffit}.
We train on 8 adaptation episodes (for the U-Net about 1 A4000 GPU-hour per map for 4k updates) and score the non-EMA weights at 0, 50, 100, 150, 250, 500, 1k, 2k, 4k and 8k updates (PixArt-$\alpha$ to 4k; SD~3.5 at 0, 250, 500, 1k, 2k and 4k), with the control checks (forgetting, the scene PSNR on the training maps, and the directional check) at 0 and 8k. 4k updates is the elbow of the curve, and we report it everywhere: across the 13 unseen maps it captures most of the benefit (on average 96 percent of each map's 8k gain; Figure~\ref{fig:adapt}) at the smallest budget; 8k serves as the check. As a baseline we fine-tune all 860.5M parameters of the U-Net at $2\times10^{-5}$ on maps 6, 7, 8 and 16 with the same eight episodes and score it at 4k.
A map's budget is the first grid point at which it recovers at least half of its zero-shot LPIPS rise over the training maps; maps that do not by 8k are right-censored~\citep{taylor2009transfer}.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\textbf{Zero-shot evaluation on unseen maps.} On unseen maps the turn response survives (U-Net directional scores 0.73 to 0.86 per map against 0.79 to 0.91 in distribution; Table~\ref{tab:unseen}).
The appearance does not: the U-Net's scene PSNR falls from 25.2~dB on the training maps to a median 22.3~dB (20.5 to 24.7 per map) and its LPIPS rises from 0.158 to 0.303 (0.229 to 0.401).
Pooled over the 13 unseen maps, one predicted frame in five has an LPIPS above 0.4, against one in 73 on the training maps; such frames concentrate in maps 16, 17, 13 and 1 (32 to 59 percent of their windows), while seven maps stay below 10 percent.
The decoder is not the limit: it reconstructs the unseen maps nearly as well as the training maps (median 27.3 against 28.6~dB, LPIPS 0.05 to 0.09).
Architecture does not change what survives the shift: in distribution the U-Net's and PixArt-$\alpha$'s scene PSNR differs by 0.02~dB and their LPIPS by 0.001, and the three backbones lose the same things (Table~\ref{tab:unseen}; \appref{app:distance}): scene PSNR drops 2.9, 2.8 and 3.3~dB and LPIPS rises by 0.145, 0.126 and 0.136, so across our three backbones the shift looks like a property of the task, not of the architecture or the scale.
It is a step, not a slope: every unseen map has a worse LPIPS than every training map for all three backbones (\appref{app:distance}).
What the model keeps is how actions act on the world: zero-shot on map 7 the weapon fires and the view advances as in the ground truth (Figure~\ref{fig:teaser}); the loss sits in the scene, whose rows fall 2.9~dB while the map-independent HUD rows fall 0.2~dB (29.7 to 29.5).

\textbf{Adaptation cost.}
Eight episodes and 4k updates raise the median scene PSNR from 22.3 to 23.6~dB and lower the median LPIPS from 0.303 to 0.210 (Figure~\ref{fig:adapt}, Table~\ref{tab:cost}); the 8k check adds little (23.7~dB, 0.204).
A map's budget, the first of the grid reads (50, 100, 150, 250, 500, 1k, 2k, 4k and 8k updates) at which it recovers half of its zero-shot rise in LPIPS over the training maps, has a median of 250 updates (100 to 8k per map, none censored; 12 of the 13 maps cross by 4k), and half of each map's 8k gain arrives within 100 updates on average.
By the same 4k updates it recovers a median 72 percent of each map's rise in LPIPS over the training maps (at least 48 percent) and a median 48 percent of its lost scene PSNR (over 100 percent on maps 8, 9 and 10, whose static recordings already sit near the training maps' PSNR).
About a decibel of the PSNR still missing against the training maps is the decoder's own reconstruction drop on the unseen maps (27.3 against 28.6~dB), which no adapter of the world model can recover.
The procedure carries over unchanged to the other backbones.
On PixArt-$\alpha$ the adapter raises the median scene PSNR on the 13 unseen maps from 22.4 to 23.7~dB and lowers LPIPS from 0.285 to 0.205 by 4k: it recovers a median 76 percent of the rise in LPIPS and 48 percent of the lost PSNR with a median budget of 250 updates (map 1 censored at its 4k grid).
On SD~3.5 the same adapter raises the median scene PSNR on the 13 unseen maps from 22.1 to 24.0~dB and lowers LPIPS from 0.262 to 0.165 by 4k: it recovers a median 77 percent of the rise in LPIPS over its in-distribution 0.126 (at least 54 percent) and 50 percent of the lost PSNR.
Its median budget is also 250 updates, but nine of its 13 maps cross at 250, the first point of its grid, so the rule cannot resolve finer there.
A full fine-tune of all 860M parameters on the same eight episodes does better on each of the four maps 6, 7, 8 and 16: at 4k it reaches a median scene PSNR of 23.1~dB and LPIPS of 0.182 against the adapter's 22.9~dB and 0.209 (0.20 to 0.29~dB and 0.017 to 0.030 in LPIPS per map) and recovers a median 87 percent of the rise in LPIPS against the adapter's 71. It trains 205 times as many parameters and needs a 49~GB RTX A6000, on which 4k updates take a median 0.8 hours, where the adapter trains on a 16~GB A4000 in 1.1 hours.
Data matters less than the first updates: in the ablation over the number of episodes (four maps), one episode already gives most of the gain of sixteen (\appref{app:perarena-adapt}).
The adapters trade some in-distribution skill for it: the training maps' scene PSNR against the decoded ground truth falls on all 13 unseen maps at the 8k check (median 0.59~dB; stock decoder), while the directional score rises from 0.81 to 0.84.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:limits}

The claims cover 13 unseen maps of one game, one agent, one-tic scoring and mostly one seed.
One-tic quality does not guarantee stable rollouts (\appref{app:closedloop}).
Next are other Doom maps and other games, to see whether the same procedure transfers; detecting a shift from the model's own zero-shot scores so that it repairs itself; closed-loop evaluation of the adapted models; and agents trained inside the adapted model.
The pattern we measure, control kept, appearance lost, most of it relearned from a few episodes, is what a deployed world model would need to detect and repair on its own, and DoomShift is a testbed for that loop.
\label{body-end}
