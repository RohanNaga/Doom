\begin{abstract}
World models have become capable simulators, but they are tested primarily in the domain they were trained on.
A small domain shift, a new arena of the same game, makes their outputs drift and lose realism, though the physics is unchanged and only the situation differs.
DoomShift asks how quickly and cheaply a world model can be adapted across such a shift: one Doom level pack has 17 arenas; we train three pretrained diffusion backbones on four and score each of the other thirteen zero-shot for all three and after adaptation for the U-Net.
On the unseen arenas the U-Net's scene PSNR falls from 25.2~dB in distribution to a median 22.3~dB and its LPIPS rises from 0.158 to 0.303, while its turn response survives; the other two backbones lose the same way.
Rank-16 adapters trained on eight episodes raise the median scene PSNR to 23.7~dB and lower LPIPS to 0.204 within 8k updates, and by 4k every arena has closed at least half of the gap that the shift opened to the reconstruction upper bound.
\end{abstract}

\keywords{World models, Domain shift, Few-shot adaptation}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Game world models now render playable worlds from pixels and controls: GameNGen runs Doom inside a diffusion model~\citep{valevski2024gamengen}, DIAMOND learns Atari and Counter-Strike in pixel space~\citep{alonso2024diamond}, and Matrix-Game~2 scales the recipe to open worlds~\citep{he2025matrixgame2}.
MultiGen trains one Doom model on 100 procedurally generated maps with a pre-trained agent~\citep{po2026multigen}, the closest prior work to ours; it scores PSNR, SSIM and LPIPS on its training distribution and reports no map outside it.
Each operates in the one environment it was trained on and is judged only there: its score is a held-out trajectory of its training scenes, and none is shown to generalize to a new environment of the same game.

A learned simulator is only useful where it is faithful, and a robot or an agent that moves to a new room, level or workspace needs to know how much of its world model still holds there and what it costs to make the rest hold.
Game worlds let us ask that question with the engine held fixed and only the environment changed.
A new arena keeps the physics and the controls but changes the layout, textures and lighting, and a model trained on a few maps renders it in the appearance it knows; training a model per map costs too much, so the question is how cheaply post-training closes the gap.
We ask: when a world model meets a new arena of the same game, what does it keep, what does it lose, and how many episodes and updates does it take to relearn the rest?

This paper turns that question into a benchmark: 17 arenas of one Doom level pack, three pretrained diffusion backbones trained on four of them, and the other thirteen scored zero-shot and after post-training.
We contribute:
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0pt}
\item \textbf{DoomShift}, the benchmark and its dataset: every-tic recordings of \prov{6,000} episodes on the four training maps and 24 on each of the 13 unseen arenas (this paper trains on 2,000 of them), with the three trained models, the decoder fine-tune and the adapter path released (Figure~\ref{fig:method}; link withheld for review).
\item A post-training recipe that adapts a frozen model to a new arena from eight episodes with a small adapter, and the per-arena protocol that scores the budget it needs.
\item The finding that it generalizes without heavy retraining: within 8k updates the median scene PSNR on the unseen arenas rises from 22.3 to 23.7~dB and LPIPS falls from 0.303 to 0.204, and all 13 arenas close half of the excess gap over the in-distribution gap by 4k (Figure~\ref{fig:adapt}; full fine-tune comparator \tbd{}).
\item The measured shift: on unseen arenas all three backbones lose scene PSNR and LPIPS in the same way while the turn response survives (Figures~\ref{fig:teaser} and~\ref{fig:shift}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Prior work}
\label{sec:related}

Held-out evaluation of world models has meant a new embodiment (concurrent work~\citep{chen2026xeworld}), a new game or environment~\citep{rigter2024avid,gao2025adaworld} or a new building~\citep{koh2021pathdreamer}; to our knowledge, no published game world model is scored on unseen maps of the same game (Genie, like the models above, reports its scores on held-out videos of its training distribution~\citep{bruce2024genie}), and Doom map holdouts exist only for agents~\citep{lample2017arnold,wydmuch2018vizdoom}.
Pretrained video models have been moved to new domains by a short full fine-tune (AdaWorld~\citep{gao2025adaworld}) or by a small adapter on a frozen model (AVID~\citep{rigter2024avid}); Vista adds action control to a frozen backbone with LoRA~\citep{gao2024vista}.
We measure the budget a small adapter needs per scene.
Persistence, the last frame copied forward, is the standard reference of video prediction~\citep{mathieu2016deep,lotter2017prednet,villegas2019fidelity}; we use it only to show why absolute PSNR alone does not compare scenes.
The reconstruction upper bound, the decoder applied to the ground-truth latent, is in practice the best a latent model rendered through that decoder can score~\citep{zheng2023occworld,karypidis2024dinoforesight}, and we read every score against it.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:bench}

\textbf{Data.} Every episode is a ViZDoom~\citep{kempka2016vizdoom} deathmatch, a free-for-all match in which each player scores by killing the others, on one of the 17 arenas of the Arnold agent's level pack~\citep{lample2017arnold}.
A level pack is a Doom WAD file that bundles a set of maps, and an arena is one of its maps, with its own layout, textures and lighting.
Arnold plays against 8 bots for 150~s, recorded at every tic, the engine's 1/35~s simulation step, as 320$\times$240 frames with the 19 executed buttons.
Of the 17 arenas, four (2 to 5, 500 episodes each) are the training maps, scored on 25 validation episodes each (512 windows); the other thirteen (1, 6 to 17), never trained on, each have 24 new episodes, 16 for adaptation and 8 held out (256 windows); in total 2,412 episodes, about 96 hours of play and about 12.1 million frames.

\textbf{Three backbones, one training recipe.} The SD~1.4 U-Net~\citep{rombach2022ldm} (860M parameters) is the anchor: it is GameNGen's backbone~\citep{valevski2024gamengen}, so our in-distribution numbers come from the same network as the published Doom result.
PixArt-$\alpha$~\citep{chen2023pixart} asks the architecture question at fixed scale, a 628M transformer instead of a U-Net in the same latent space, and architecture does not change what survives the shift (in distribution their scene PSNR differs by 0.02~dB; on unseen arenas their median PSNR falls to 22.30 and 22.38~dB and their LPIPS rises to 0.303 and 0.285; Table~\ref{tab:unseen}).
SD~3.5 Medium~\citep{esser2024sd3}, a widely adopted open MMDiT (2.27B parameters, 16-channel latents), asks the scale question; its latent space changes with it, so that step compares pretrained packages, not architectures.
All three become next-tic world models under one recipe (Figure~\ref{fig:method}): 32 context tics so the model sees motion, $v$-prediction~\citep{salimans2022progressive} for stable training across noise levels, and context noise augmentation as in GameNGen~\citep{valevski2024gamengen} so the model tolerates its own rollout errors.
Each trains for 200k updates of batch 32, a step count matched across backbones, keeps an EMA at 0.9999 in fp32 because a bf16 EMA underflows and freezes, and is sampled with 10-step DDIM~\citep{song2021ddim} so that evaluation stays cheap (\appref{app:protocol}).

\textbf{What we measure.} We score each predicted frame one tic ahead against the raw ground-truth frame on the scene rows 0 to 207, with PSNR and LPIPS~\citep{zhang2018lpips}; the HUD rows below them do not depend on the map.
Absolute PSNR alone does not compare arenas, because it rises with how static a recording is: copying the last context frame forward, the persistence baseline of video prediction~\citep{mathieu2016deep}, already scores 19.8~dB on the unseen arenas (18.5 to 22.5 per arena).
We therefore read every arena against two references: the \emph{reconstruction upper bound}, the decoder $D$ applied to the ground-truth latent, which is in practice the best a prediction rendered through $D$ can score (median 27.3~dB on the unseen arenas, 28.6 on the training maps), and the in-distribution level, the same model on the training maps; the \emph{gap to the upper bound} is the upper bound's PSNR minus the prediction's.
The \emph{directional} score checks the controls: the fraction of turning windows whose predicted view turns the other way when left and right are swapped in the newest control.
The zero-shot latent skill score $S_0$ (\appref{app:skill}) scores the model in its own latent space before any decoding.

\textbf{Training setup.} Each backbone trains on one RTX A6000 (49~GB) in bf16 with fused AdamW, at 1.8 updates per second for the U-Net, 1.4 for PixArt-$\alpha$ and 0.53 for SD~3.5; each adapter trains on one RTX A4000 (16~GB) at about 1.0 updates per second.

\textbf{Decoder.} Every backbone is trained on latents from its frozen encoder, so the latent space is the contract between encoder, world model and decoder: changing the encoder would move every training target and invalidate the trained backbones, the adapters and the reconstruction upper bound.
The decoder only renders: it sits after the model, and its own error is what the reconstruction upper bound measures, so fine-tuning it changes what we can see, not what the model knows.
We fine-tune the SD~1 decoder $D$ on training-map frames paired with their frozen-encoder latents, using MSE plus 0.1~LPIPS for 3,486 updates and gating it on held-out training-map frames (\appref{app:protocol}); it never sees an unseen arena.
On 200 validation frames it raises the scene reconstruction from 26.9 to 28.4~dB and lowers its LPIPS from 0.092 to 0.060, and it reconstructs the unseen arenas nearly as well as the training maps (median 27.3 against 28.6~dB) while the model's prediction loses 2.9~dB, so the shift lives in the model, not the renderer; the LPIPS term is there because MSE alone more than triples scene LPIPS (0.092 to 0.339).
We use it for every pixel score of the U-Net and PixArt-$\alpha$ unless stated otherwise; SD~3.5 uses its own stock decoder.

\textbf{In-distribution performance.} On the training maps the three backbones reach a scene PSNR of 25.20, 25.18 and \prov{24.50}~dB and an LPIPS of 0.158, 0.159 and \prov{0.146} one tic ahead, 3.4, 3.4 and \prov{5.5}~dB below their decoders' reconstruction upper bounds, and turn the right way (directional 0.84 to 0.86 against 0.885 for the ground-truth frames; Table~\ref{tab:unseen}).
Four tics ahead they reach 21.33, 21.29 and \prov{21.59}~dB (stock decoders, full frame; SD~3.5 at 140k).

\textbf{Post-training recipe.} To move a trained model to a new arena we keep its weights frozen and train a small adapter on a few episodes of that arena, then score the adapted model on held-out episodes at each update budget.
For each unseen arena we adapt the U-Net 200k EMA weights with rank-16 LoRA~\citep{hu2022lora} on every attention projection of the frozen backbone~\citep{gao2024vista} and train the control MLP, input projection and noise-bucket embedding in full (4.2M parameters, 0.49 percent)~\citep{xie2023difffit}.
We train on 8 adaptation episodes (1.1 A4000 GPU-hours per arena for 4k updates) and score the non-EMA weights at 0, 50, 100, 150, 250, 500, 1k, 2k, 4k and 8k updates, with the control checks (forgetting, the scene PSNR on the training maps, and the directional check) at 0 and 8k; a full fine-tune comparator at $2\times10^{-5}$ is in progress (\tbd{}).
An arena's budget is the first grid point at which it closes half of its excess gap over the in-distribution gap to the reconstruction upper bound, that is, at which its gap to the upper bound falls to the midpoint between its zero-shot gap and the training maps' 3.4~dB; arenas that do not by 8k are right-censored~\citep{taylor2009transfer}.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

We report what a model loses zero-shot, what post-training recovers and with what budget, and what predicts how far an arena gets.

\textbf{Zero-shot: where the deficit sits.} What does a model keep and what does it lose when it meets an arena it never saw? On unseen arenas the turn response survives (U-Net reversal rates 0.73 to 0.86 per arena against 0.79 to 0.91 in distribution; Table~\ref{tab:unseen}).
The appearance does not: the U-Net's scene PSNR falls from 25.2~dB on the training maps to a median 22.3~dB (20.5 to 24.7 per arena), its LPIPS rises from 0.158 to 0.303 (0.229 to 0.401), and its gap to the reconstruction upper bound grows from 3.4 to a median 5.0~dB (3.7 to 6.9).
The decoder is not the limit: it reconstructs the unseen arenas nearly as well as the training maps (median 27.3 against 28.6~dB, LPIPS 0.05 to 0.09).
The three backbones lose the same things (Table~\ref{tab:unseen}, Figure~\ref{fig:shift}): the shift looks like a property of the task, not of the architecture or the scale.
It is a step, not a slope: every unseen arena has a lower zero-shot skill score $S_0$ and a worse LPIPS than every training map for all three backbones (\appref{app:distance}).
What the model keeps is how actions act on the world: zero-shot on arena 7 the weapon fires and the view advances as in the ground truth (Figure~\ref{fig:teaser}) and the turn response holds; the loss sits in the scene, whose rows fall 2.9~dB (25.2 to a median 22.3) while the map-independent HUD rows fall 0.2~dB (29.7 to 29.5).

\textbf{Post-training: what it takes.} We next adapt a small subset of the U-Net's parameters on each unseen arena and ask what it takes to recover what was lost.
Eight episodes and 8k updates raise the median scene PSNR from 22.3 to 23.7~dB and lower the median LPIPS from 0.303 to 0.204, and the median gap to the reconstruction upper bound falls from 5.0 to 3.4~dB, the in-distribution level (Figure~\ref{fig:adapt}, Table~\ref{tab:cost}).
Most of the PSNR still missing against the training maps (1.5~dB) reflects the arenas' lower upper bound (27.3 against 28.6~dB), not the model.
All 13 arenas close half of their excess gap over the in-distribution gap by 4k, the first budget scored against the raw frame (budgets below 4k \tbd{}), and on average 96 percent of each arena's 8k gain is in place by 4k.
Data matters less than the first updates: on the four arenas of the data ladder, one episode already gives most of the gain of sixteen (\appref{app:perarena-adapt}).
The adapters trade some in-distribution skill for it: the training maps' scene PSNR falls on \tbd{} of 13 arenas (median \tbd{}~dB), while the directional score rises from 0.81 to 0.84.

\textbf{What predicts the outcome.} Neither the zero-shot skill score nor a registered distance orders how far an arena gets in raw terms: $S_0$ correlates only weakly with the 8k gap to the reconstruction upper bound ($+0.38$, $p=0.20$) and with the PSNR gain ($+0.43$, $p=0.14$), and a frame distance $d$ between an arena's recordings and the nearest training map, registered before any scoring, separates the training maps from every unseen arena but orders none of the 13 (Spearman $-0.07$ with the zero-shot PSNR; \appref{app:distance}).

\section{Future work}
\label{sec:limits}

On unseen arenas a Doom world model keeps its turn response and its action effects and loses the arena's appearance; eight episodes and at most 8k adapter updates raise its median scene PSNR from 22.3 to 23.7~dB and lower its LPIPS from 0.303 to 0.204, closing half of the excess gap over the in-distribution gap on all 13 arenas by 4k.
The claims cover 13 arenas of one level pack, one agent, one-tic scoring and mostly one seed.
One-tic quality does not guarantee stable rollouts: SD~3.5, best at one tic on the full frame with stock decoders, collapses in 23 non-EMA and 9 EMA rollouts of 256, and the EMA removes checkpoint-specific collapses, not the failure mode (\appref{app:closedloop}).
Next are other level packs and other games, to see whether the same recipe transfers; detecting a shift from the model's own zero-shot skill score so that it repairs itself; closed-loop evaluation of the adapted models; and agents trained inside the adapted model.
The pattern we measure, control kept, appearance lost, most of it relearned from a few episodes, is what a deployed world model would need to detect and repair on its own, and DoomShift is a testbed for that loop.
