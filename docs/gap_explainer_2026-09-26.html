Where the unseen-map gap lives

What "rendering term of a decomposition" means, what the three reviews concluded, and what to think about next. Numbers: U-Net 200k EMA, one tic ahead, 30 evaluation maps, frozen in the repo (Sep 26 2026).

1. The pipeline, and the three places a prediction can go wrong

1 · Control response
Does the backbone apply the control correctly in a room it never saw? The directional check swaps the two turn bits in the newest control and asks whether the predicted camera motion reverses by the right amount. It measures yaw under a turn, not all of the game's physics: not forward motion, doors, or enemies. On training maps both models score about 0.85; the 30-map run tonight gives the per-map curve.
2 · Latent prediction error
How far the predicted latent is from the true one, compared with the trivial guess of repeating the last latent, all in latent space before any decoder is involved. This is the backbone's own error on unfamiliar footage: new textures, new geometry, new monsters, entangled. It is the decoder-free version of "gain over persistence" and is being rescored on all 30 maps tonight.
3 · Rendering
Decode the true latent of the next frame and score it against the real pixels. That is the decoder's reconstruction ceiling. If the ceiling itself dropped on unseen maps, the decoder would be part of the problem and tuning it on the new map would help. Tuning the decoder on training maps (running tonight) raises the ceiling for every map at once; it cannot change the latent the backbone predicted.

Read together: the decoder renders unseen maps about as well as training maps, so a decoder tuned on an unseen map has at most a few tenths of a dB of genuine rendering loss to recover. The loss is in the predicted latents. That is why the Sunday "adapt the decoder to the new map" experiment was cancelled: it could only have improved the painting of a wrong prediction. One caveat from Astra: the ceiling is measured on true latents, and a decoder can behave differently on off-distribution predicted latents, so the paired experiment (decode the same predictions with the stock and the tuned decoder) is what settles the rendering leg rather than this table alone.

Three campaign maps (19, 25, 26) have a ceiling below persistence itself: their footage is so static that copy-last-frame beats even a perfect prediction through this autoencoder. So the headline against persistence leads with LPIPS, where all 26 unseen maps lose and the ceiling is fine, and every PSNR is shown with its ceiling.

4. Where the distance metric fits

The distance is the x axis of everything above. It says how far a map's footage sits from the nearest training map, measured three ways (SD 1.x latents, pixels, SD 3.5 latents; the rankings agree at 0.86 to 0.90), and it is frozen before any score was read. What it is good for: ordering the 30 maps so that each leg of the decomposition can be read as a curve, and predicting which maps will need adaptation. What it cannot yet do: prove that distance causes the drop, because with 30 maps in three clusters (training arenas, other arenas, campaign) the pooled correlation cannot be separated from the cluster structure. It stays in the paper as qualified evidence with that stated, and becomes a validated predictor only when it predicts held-out maps out of sample, which needs more maps.
